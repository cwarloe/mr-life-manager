Mr. Life Manager

You can't see your desk anymore.

Drawings, binders, parts, cables, and the stack you've been meaning to get to. Here's how to do it: one surface, about two hours, and it's actually finished.

Pick one surface. Not the whole office. The desk, or the table, or the shelf behind you. A whole office takes a week you won't find. One desk is an afternoon, and then it's done.

Once you start, finish the surface, all the way to the Return box being empty. A desk that's half sorted is worse than one you haven't touched. That's why it's one surface and not the whole office: small enough to finish today.

Nothing to sign up for. Start at one.

Ready to start? It takes about two hours.

You need to be at your desk, with the time blocked off. If you're somewhere else, or now isn't the time, pick the exact time you'll do it.

  1. Decide now what you'll do when you want to quit.

    10 min

    These are the ones that will show up. You will hear yourself say them.

    • "I might need that for a future project."
    • "I don't know whose document this is."
    • "I'll scan these receipts later."
    • "Let me just check Slack or email real quick."

    Remove the reasons to quit before you start. Get a big trash bag, a recycling bin, and one empty box labeled "Return." Locate the shredder bin and make sure the file room isn't locked. Close your email and chat apps.

    Knock out the roadblocks before you start. People don't quit because clearing a desk is hard. They quit when they hit an excuse—like a jammed shredder or a locked file room. Clear the obstacles out of your way before you start.

  2. Clear the desk surface and sort into three piles.

    45 min

    Start with a Desk Zero sweep: move everything loose off the desk surface into staging bins on the floor, and wipe the bare desktop clean. Then sort items one by one into three piles: trash / recycle, return (belongs to someone else, the lab, or the archive), or stays here.

    A bare desk breaks the cycle. When you leave clutter on the surface while sorting, you just end up shuffling piles around. Wiping the desk bare first gives you an immediate clean workspace, and only items that earn daily reach status get to go back on it.

  3. Use the 5-Second Title Rule for every piece of paper.

    —

    Never read past the header. Read the title or first line, and make the call: if it lives digitally or on the server, recycle the paper copy. If it's an official signed or sealed record, put it in the Return box for archiving. If it's a 60-second action, do it right now.

    Reading paper is a procrastination trap. The moment you start reading paragraphs of old memos or project notes, your sorting session is dead. Read only the title, decide where it belongs, and keep moving.

  4. Clear the piles out of the office today.

    20 min

    Trash and recycling out. Walk the Return box around: hand things back, drop records at the file room, return samples and tools to where they came from. The box should be empty before you go home.

    A pile left in the office is not progress. It's a pile. This step decides whether the desk stays clear or fills back up over the next two weeks. If you only have time for part of it, do the Return box, because that's the pile that ends up back on your desk.

  5. Put back what's left, by how often you use it.

    25 min

    Your daily tools go right where your hand lands naturally. Project archives and reference binders go on the top shelf with labeled spines. Cables, adapters, and small hardware go into clear plastic bins labeled on the front.

    Zero brainpower ergonomics. You should be able to reach for your primary tools half-asleep without hunting. And you should be able to identify what's in every bin from across the room so you stop ordering duplicate cables.

  6. Give new paper one place to land, then stop.

    10 min

    Put one tray at the edge of the desk. From now on, everything new goes in the tray, not on the desk. Wipe the surface down. Then stop. Don't start on the shelves today.

    New paper needs one place to go, or it goes everywhere. One tray is that place. The desk surface is for the work in front of you today, not for storage.

Two things worth knowing

Before you shred or toss anything with a project number, a signature, a seal, or a client's name on it, check your firm's records rule. Some documents have to be kept for years. When you're not sure, it goes in the Return box for the file room, not the trash.

Old batteries, monitors, and electronics don't go in the office trash. Ask facilities or IT where they go. IT may also need to wipe any drive before it leaves.

One surface. That's a finished thing.

You can see the desk, you can find what you need, and nothing is waiting in a pile for a decision you already made.

The shelves are a different afternoon. They'll be faster, because you've already made most of the hard decisions about what you keep.

Why it filled up in the first place.

Almost everything on that desk had no home. It wasn't trash and it wasn't finished, so it went down "for now," and the desk became the place where "for now" happens.

That isn't a discipline problem. It's an unmade decision, repeated every day for months. That's why straightening never fixed it. Straightening moves things, and the problem was never where they were.

Give everything a place. Deal with it now.

What just happened

You cleared one surface completely.

The rule is to reset once, then maintain, and that maintenance means nothing until the reset has happened. Most advice skips straight to the routine, which is why it never kept a desk like this one clear.

The maintenance is small: the last ten minutes of the day, empty the tray and put things back where they live.
