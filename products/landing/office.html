Mr. Life Manager

You can't see your desk anymore.

Drawings, binders, parts, cables, and the stack you've been meaning to get to. Here's how Dave does it: one surface, about two hours, and it's actually finished.

Pick one surface. Not the whole office. The desk, or the table, or the shelf behind you. A whole office is a week you'll keep not having. One desk is an afternoon, and then it's done.

Do the steps in order and stop whenever you need to. The order puts the decisions first, so stopping early still leaves you further ahead than any other order would.

Nothing to sign up for. Start at one.

Ready to start?

The steps are below either way. These just clear everything else out of the way.

  1. Put two hours on your calendar.

    5 min

    Block it like a meeting, ideally at the end of a day or on a Friday afternoon. Set your status to busy. If someone will wonder where you are, tell them you're clearing your desk.

    Why it comes first. An office only gets cleaned in the gaps between real work, and there are no gaps. If it isn't scheduled, the next urgent email wins every time. Half-finished is worse than not started: you lose the desk and gain a pile of decisions you've already made once.

  2. Close the exits.

    10 min

    These are the ones that will show up. You will hear yourself say them.

    • "I might need that for the project."
    • "I don't know whose this is."
    • "I'll scan it later."
    • "Let me just answer this email first."

    So close the actual exits first. Get a big trash bag, a recycling bin, and one empty box labeled "Return." Find out where the shred bin and the records or archive room are. Close your email.

    This is the part everyone skips, and it's the part Dave cares most about. His line is "we don't want any excuses that the dump isn't open." People rarely quit because the job is hard. They quit at the first reasonable-sounding obstacle, so you remove the obstacles before you start.

  3. Three piles. Nothing else.

    45 min

    Start at one corner of the surface and work across. Everything goes into exactly one of three piles: throw away or recycle, return (it belongs to someone else, the lab, the shop, or the archive), or stays here. Anything staying stays put for now.

    Three is the whole method. A fourth pile called "decide later" is how the desk got this way. Every stack on it is a decision that got postponed, and adding one more stack doesn't change that.

  4. When you get stuck on paper: is there another copy?

    —

    If the current version lives on the server or in the project file, the printout on your desk can go. If it's a record (signed, stamped, sealed, or the only copy), it goes in the Return box for the file room. If it's a task that takes a minute, do it now. Don't negotiate with yourself for more than about five seconds per item.

    Most paper on an engineer's desk is a copy of something that lives somewhere else. Old markups, superseded revisions, meeting handouts. Once the real one exists, the copy on your desk does nothing except hide what you need. "Do it now" is the second of Dave's two rules.

  5. Take the piles away now. Today.

    20 min

    Trash and recycling out. Walk the Return box around: hand things back, drop records at the file room, return samples and tools to where they came from. The box should be empty before you go home.

    A pile left in the office is not progress. It's a pile. This step decides whether the work holds or quietly undoes itself over the next two weeks. If you only have time for part of it, do the Return box, because that's the pile that drifts back onto your desk.

  6. Put back what's left, in Dave's order.

    25 min

    Most-used things within arm's reach. Least-used things highest or furthest away. Like with like. Binders upright with the spines labeled. Parts, cables, and hardware in clear bins, labeled on the side facing out.

    His test: you should be able to tell what's in every bin and binder from across the room. That's what stops a cleared desk from filling up again. You can see what you have, so you stop burying it and stop ordering it twice.

  7. Give new paper one place to land, then stop.

    10 min

    Put one tray at the edge of the desk. From now on, everything new goes in the tray, not on the desk. Wipe the surface down. Then stop. Don't start on the shelves today.

    Dave once told me I needed to "find a place in your office for mail." One tray is that place. The desk surface is for the work in front of you today, the same way he says the dining room table is not for storage.

Two things worth knowing

Before you shred or toss anything with a project number, a signature, a seal, or a client's name on it, check your firm's records rule. Some documents have to be kept for years. When you're not sure, it goes in the Return box for the file room, not the trash.

Old batteries, monitors, and electronics don't go in the office trash. Ask facilities or IT where they go. IT may also need to wipe any drive before it leaves.

One surface. That's a finished thing.

You can see the desk, you can find what you need, and nothing is waiting in a pile for a decision you already made.

The shelves are a different afternoon. They'll be faster, because you've already made most of the hard calls about what you keep.

Why it filled up in the first place.

Almost everything on that desk had no home. It wasn't trash and it wasn't finished, so it went down "for now," and the desk became the place where "for now" happens.

That isn't a discipline problem. It's an unmade decision, repeated every day for months. That's why straightening never fixed it. Straightening moves things, and the problem was never where they were.

"Everything has it's place. Do it now."

Those are Dave's. He's a real person, he's been saying them for decades, and he taught me how to run a house after I'd spent years failing at it. The method on this page is the one he wrote out for my garage in 2009. It works on a desk for the same reason it works on a garage.

What just happened

You brought one surface back to zero.

Dave's rule is that you reset once, then maintain, and that maintenance means nothing until the reset has happened. Most advice skips straight to the routine, which is why it never stuck on a desk like this one.

The maintenance is small: the last ten minutes of the day, empty the tray and put things back where they live.

Want to keep it this way?

I'll send one thing worth printing: Dave's week on one page. It's written for a home, but the top of it, ten minutes at the end of the day putting everything back where it lives, is exactly what keeps a desk clear. Two shorter follow-ups come after that, and then they stop.
