Mr. Life Manager

There's a room you stopped using.

Garage, spare room, basement, a closet you don't open. Here's how to do it: one zone, about two hours, and it's actually finished.

Pick one zone. Not the whole room. One wall, one corner, one closet. A whole garage takes a day you won't find. One wall takes an afternoon, and then it's done.

Once you start, finish the zone, all the way to the piles leaving the room. A zone that's half sorted is worse than one you haven't touched. That's why it's one zone and not the whole room: small enough to finish today.

Nothing to sign up for. Start at one.

Ready to start? It takes about two hours.

You need to be in the room, with about two hours to finish one zone. If you're somewhere else, or now isn't the time, pick the exact time you'll do it.

  1. Decide now what you'll do when you want to quit.

    15 min

    These are the ones that will show up. You will hear yourself say them.

    • "This is going to take all day."
    • "There's too much stuff to sort."
    • "I'll finish going through this tomorrow."
    • "I need to look through these photos first."
    • "I should sell this on eBay."

    Remove the excuses before you open the door. Find two donation drop-offs and check their hours today. Have heavy-duty contractor trash bags ready. Clear out your car trunk so you can load donation bags immediately.

    Knock out the roadblocks before you start. People don't quit because sorting is hard. They quit the second they hit an excuse—like a closed thrift store or running out of trash bags. Remove every barrier up front.

  2. Sort the zone in three 12-minute sprints.

    45 min

    Do not try to power through 45 minutes straight. Run three focused 12-minute sprints with a 2-minute water break between each. Everything in your zone goes into exactly one pile: throw away, give away, or belongs elsewhere in the house. Keepers in this room stay put for now.

    Three piles only—never create a "maybe" pile. A "decide later" pile just guarantees the room fills right back up. Parting with old items feels weirdly personal. Be honest about what you actually use versus what is just stealing your square footage.

  3. Use the 5-second test on every item you hesitate on.

    —

    Ask: "Have I used this in the past 12 months?" If no, it goes into give away or throw away. Real tools and equipment you actually use stay. Make every call in under five seconds.

    Overthinking creates clutter. If you have to talk yourself into keeping something with a hypothetical scenario ("I might need this if I take up camping in two years"), you don't need it. Let it go.

  4. Get the piles out of the room right now.

    30 min

    Trash straight to the dumpster. Donations directly into your car trunk—not stacked in the hallway. Items that belong elsewhere in the house get carried directly to the room they live in.

    The Trunk Off-Ramp. Once donation bags are loaded into your car trunk, you are 100% finished with them for today. Even if the thrift store is closed, they are out of your living space. Set an alarm on your phone to drop them off tomorrow on your commute.

  5. Put back what's left, by how often you use it.

    25 min

    Everyday tools go right at eye level and arm's reach. Stuff you use once a year goes up top. Keep like with like in clear plastic bins labeled on the front.

    Kill the black holes. Cardboard boxes bury things forever. Clear bins let you see what you own from across the room. Put things in the same spot every time so you can grab what you need in your sleep without tearing through piles.

  6. Sweep the floor, then stop.

    10 min

    Sweep the floor. Empty the trash can, and put a spare bag in the bottom of it before the one you're using. Then stop. Don't start the next zone today.

    Then do something you enjoy. The job has an end, and you should feel it. Rolling straight into the next zone is how one good afternoon becomes a chore you avoid.

Two things worth knowing

If you find paint, solvents, batteries or old electronics, those don't go in household trash. Most areas have a drop-off; it's worth a search before you load the car.

Lift with your legs and don't carry anything down a ladder. Genuinely the most common way this afternoon goes wrong.

One zone. That's a finished thing.

You can see the floor, you can see what you own, and nothing is waiting in a pile for a decision you already made.

The next zone is a different afternoon. It'll be faster, because you've already made most of the hard decisions about what you keep.

Why it filled up in the first place.

Almost everything in that room had no home. It wasn't trash and it wasn't in use, so it went somewhere "for now" — and the room became the place where "for now" happens.

That's not a discipline problem, it's an unmade decision repeated for years. Which is why tidying never fixed it: tidying moves things, and the problem was never where they were.

Give everything a place. Deal with it now.

What just happened

You cleared one zone completely.

The rule is to reset once, then maintain — and that maintenance is meaningless until the reset has happened. Most advice skips straight to the routine, which is why it doesn't work on a room like this one.
