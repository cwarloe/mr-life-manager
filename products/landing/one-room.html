There's a room you stopped using.

Garage, spare room, basement, a closet you don't open. Here's how Dave does it — one zone, about two hours, and it's actually finished.

Pick one zone. Not the whole room. One wall, one corner, one closet. A whole garage takes a day you won't find. One wall takes an afternoon, and then it's done.

Do the steps in order and stop whenever you need to. The order puts the decisions first, so stopping early still leaves you further ahead than any other order would.

Nothing to sign up for. Start at one.

Are you there right now?

This only works in the room itself. If you're somewhere else, pick the exact time you'll do it.

  1. Answer one question before you touch anything.

    2 min

    Dave opens every one of his guides with it: do I actually want this, and am I willing to work on it until it's done? If the answer is no today, close this and come back. That's a real answer and it costs you nothing.

    Why he asks. Half-finished is worse than not started — you lose the use of the space and you get a pile of decisions you've already made once. Deciding up front is the difference between finishing a project and moving the same clutter to a new place.

  2. Decide now what you'll do when you want to quit.

    15 min

    These are the ones that will show up. You will hear yourself say them.

    • "This will take too long."
    • "There's too much to do."
    • "I need a nap."
    • "I forgot where the trash can is."
    • "I'll do it tomorrow."

    So remove the reasons to quit before you start. Find two places that take donations and check they're open today. Work out where the trash goes and whether you can get there. Get the car empty enough to load. Have boxes or bags ready.

    This is the part everyone skips, and it's the part Dave cares most about. His line is "we don't want any excuses that the dump isn't open." People rarely quit because a job is hard — they quit at the first legitimate-sounding reason, and a genuine obstacle is the most legitimate-sounding reason there is. So you remove them before you start.

  3. Sort everything in the zone into three piles.

    45 min

    Start at one end of your zone and work across. Everything goes into exactly one of three piles: throw away, give away, belongs somewhere else in the house. Anything you're keeping right here stays where it is for now.

    Three piles are all you need. A fourth pile called "decide later" is how this turns into a room full of boxes, which is what happened the last time. Dave is blunt about this part: it's emotional for some people, so be logical and honest with yourself.

  4. When you're stuck on an item, ask whether you've used it in the past year.

    —

    If no, it goes. If it's genuinely useful and works — tools, equipment you actually use — it stays. Spend no more than about five seconds deciding on each item.

    Once you notice an excuse, it's much easier to ignore. Dave wrote the list in 2009, then added, "I'm sure you can come up with some good ones."

  5. Get the piles out of the room today.

    30 min

    Trash out. Donations into the car and gone, not stacked by the door. The third pile goes into the house and gets put away where those things live.

    A pile left in the room is not progress, it's a pile. This is the step that decides whether the room stays clear or fills back up over the next two weeks. If you only have time for part of this, do the donations — they're the ones that come back.

  6. Put back what's left, in Dave's order.

    25 min

    Least-used things go highest. Most-used at easy reach. Like with like. Loose things go in clear boxes, labeled on the side facing out.

    His test: there should be nothing on the shelves whose contents you can't identify from across the room. That's what stops a tidy space turning back into a storage space — you can see what you own, so you stop buying it twice and stop burying it.

  7. Sweep the floor, then stop.

    10 min

    Sweep the floor. Empty the trash can, and put a spare bag in the bottom of it before the one you're using. Then stop. Don't start the next zone today.

    Dave's actual last step here is "go get a beer." The point stands whatever you drink: the job has an end, and you should feel it. Rolling straight into the next zone is how one good afternoon becomes a chore you avoid.

Two things worth knowing

If you find paint, solvents, batteries or old electronics, those don't go in household trash. Most areas have a drop-off; it's worth a search before you load the car.

Lift with your legs and don't carry anything down a ladder. Genuinely the most common way this afternoon goes wrong.

One zone. That's a finished thing.

You can see the floor, you can see what you own, and nothing is waiting in a pile for a decision you already made.

The next zone is a different afternoon. It'll be faster, because you've already made most of the hard decisions about what you keep.

Why it filled up in the first place.

Almost everything in that room had no home. It wasn't trash and it wasn't in use, so it went somewhere "for now" — and the room became the place where "for now" happens.

That's not a discipline problem, it's an unmade decision repeated for years. Which is why tidying never fixed it: tidying moves things, and the problem was never where they were.

"Everything has it's place. Do it now."

Those are Dave's. He's a real person, he's been saying them for decades, and he taught me how to run a house after I'd spent years failing at it. He wrote the method on this page out for me in 2009, excuse list and all, because my garage had filled up.

What just happened

You cleared one zone completely.

Dave's rule is that you reset once, then maintain — and that maintenance is meaningless until the reset has happened. Most advice skips straight to the routine, which is why it doesn't work on a room like this one.

He closes that 2009 document with a line worth keeping: we always overestimate what we can do in a year and underestimate what we can do in ten.
