# Intervention session template

Help someone make one part of their home usable and leave them able to put it back that way.

**Primary domain:** Home. **Secondary:** Hospitality.
**Status:** Internal facilitator template, 2026-10-27; not yet tested in a new session.
Derived from the [January 2014 intervention](../source-material/2014-01-15-dave-webster-vu-intervention.md).
The prompts and forms below are our wording, not a script Dave wrote.
See the [source analysis](../source-material/2014-01-15-analysis.md) for timestamped evidence
and [Dave's principles](../frameworks/dave-principles.md) for the broader method.

## Before the visit

Agree on the help the person wants, the time available, and what would make the
visit worthwhile. Ask separately about recording and reuse. Agreeing to help
does not mean agreeing to become content. A nomination is an invitation, not an
obligation. Follow [ADR-022](../planning/decisions.md#adr-022--price-follows-what-it-costs-us-to-deliver):
clear, freely given consent, the right to refuse or withdraw, and no shaming.
Before recording a new intervention, put the consent process in writing,
including the permitted uses and how withdrawal is handled.

Keep personal session notes private; this public repository holds the blank
template and approved source material. Let the person make decisions about
their belongings. Ask before opening private storage or discarding anything.

## 1. Decide: understand the person and the space

Walk through together before prescribing a layout. Ask:

- What would you like to be able to do here?
- What do you use often? What has not been used lately?
- Does everything have to fit here, or is other storage available?
- What already works, and what gets in the way?

Choose one area small enough to leave usable within the time available. Say
what is achievable without promising a whole-home transformation.

## 2. Prepare: make room to work

Clear an easy patch of floor or a work surface first. Remove obvious trash
with the person's agreement. Keep the exit clear. The first win creates room
and makes the next decision easier; the hardest category can wait until there
is space to handle it.

Start two short lists: **actions** and **supplies**. Count or measure what is
needed before buying. A missing shelf need not stop useful sorting today;
record what is temporary and what the next visit must finish.

**Source:** 08:09–11:49, 13:25–13:58, 16:10–17:26.

## 3. Reset: sort one area and clean what is exposed

Explain these four destinations, then work through the chosen area together:

| Destination | Decision |
|---|---|
| Throw away | Is this unusable trash? |
| Give away | Could someone else use it, and does the person agree to let it go? |
| Use often | Does it need easy access in everyday life? |
| Use occasionally | Is it worth keeping, with a less prominent home? |

These are the categories Dave uses in this visit. His garage plan uses three
outbound piles. Choose categories that make decisions clear for this person;
the number is not the principle. If a decision needs information, write the
specific question and next action instead of creating a permanent mystery pile.
That last step is our facilitation adaptation.

Wipe exposed shelves before putting things back. Finish the chosen area before
opening more storage than can be restored today.

**Source:** 11:49–13:25, 19:55–21:07. Bounding the area is our session design.

## 4. Place: visible, grouped, reachable, repeatable

Put like things together. Give frequently used items the easiest useful reach,
near where they are used. Put occasional items farther away. Make contents
recognizable without rummaging: use clear containers or open groupings where
appropriate. Labels are our alternative when visibility is impractical.
Use existing storage if it works; the outcome matters more than new containers.

Ask the person to find an item and put it back. Can they see what they have,
reach it, and return it to the same home? This hands-on check is our addition
to Dave's explanation of why a consistent place saves thought and time.

**Source:** 07:13–08:39, 10:14–11:16, 15:08–15:41, 17:26–18:55,
24:31–26:19, 28:44–30:01.

## 5. Maintain: leave a usable result and a specific next action

Review what changed together. Take trash out and arrange the agreed handoffs;
staging something by the door is not the same as completing its removal.
Write the next actions with an owner and a time. Dave turns giving away a TV
into calling the recipient before bed (12:53–13:25).

Agree on a small return-to-baseline action and a follow-up time. These are our
extensions from Dave's [weekly schedule](../source-material/2009-11-04-dave-webster-weekly-schedule.md),
not events shown in this recording.

- Today's completed win: ____________________
- Still temporary or unfinished: ____________________
- How the person will put this area back after using it: ____________________
- Next check-in, and who will initiate it: ____________________

Good enough is an area that can be used, with findable homes and an explicit
plan for unfinished work. The ideal adds any storage improvements and a routine
the person can sustain. Count neither a supply list nor a planned return visit
as completed work.

## After the session: capture the lesson

Record what the person actually did and what remained hard. Keep their own
lessons distinct from the facilitator's interpretation. Reuse personal stories,
images, or recordings only within the agreed consent.

A proposed improvement is a hypothesis until another session tests it. Carry a
useful result into the existing source analysis or principles framework rather
than presenting one successful moment as proof of the whole service.
