# Vivid Vision — Mr. Life Manager, October 2029

**Status:** First draft, 2026-10-26. Shaped from Charles's own answers to twelve
prompts (given by voice and text the same day), plus what he and Dave have
already written to each other. **Charles's words are kept wherever possible.**
Anything in *[brackets and italics]* was filled in by Claude to connect the
pieces, and needs his yes or no.

**Method:** Cameron Herold's Vivid Vision: a few pages, written in the present
tense, describing what the organization looks, sounds and feels like three
years out. It says *what*, never *how*. The how belongs in the
[roadmap](../../planning/roadmap.md) and the lifecycle strategy. Like the Doxa
vision Charles helped write, it is meant to work like a magnet: the right people
are drawn to it, and the wrong ones are not.

---

It's October 2029, and Mr. Life Manager is doing what it was made to do:
people's lives are changing because they take simple things and act on them.
They make progress without feeling overwhelmed or burdened. They don't read
about getting their life in order. They do the first thing on the checklist,
and then the next one, and then it's done.

This was never about making money. It exists because a friend named Dave once
helped me make progress when I couldn't make it on my own, and I've still got a
lot of progress to go. This is me passing on what he gave me.

## The people we serve

**Jordan** saw a flier on a board outside the student union during the first
week of the semester. The kitchen in his shared house hadn't been clean since
August. He scanned the code standing there, pressed "Not right now," picked
Tuesday at 7, and on Tuesday at 7 his phone reminded him. Forty minutes later
the kitchen was done. He sent the page to two of his roommates.

**Morgan** is a second lieutenant at her first assignment. Four years at the
Academy taught her to pass an inspection; nobody taught her to set her own
standard in an apartment with no one checking. A volunteer she trusted handed
her the link the week she graduated. She worked through it the way she worked
through everything at the Academy, and she still uses the weekly schedule.

**Chris** is three years into a cybersecurity job with on-call weeks and
travel. He had bought the planner, downloaded the app and watched the videos.
None of it stuck. A coworker sent him the link with a calendar invite: "Saturday
at 10. I'll check on you." He was a little annoyed by how checklist-y it was.
He did it anyway.

What people say about Mr. Life Manager when I'm not in the room:

> "I didn't want to like it. But I tried it, and I got it done. I have to admit
> it worked better than if I'd just read something and made a plan."

They're almost a little annoyed that the checklists are so checklist-y that
they're pushed into taking action. They can't just read it and think about it.
That annoyance is the point.

## How people find us

People find Mr. Life Manager on a board at school, in a coffee shop, or on a
flier someone hands them. Churches recommend it. Mentors ask, "Have you tried
this checklist?" College ministries, landlords and apartment managers pass it to
the young people they see every day.

Most of all, people who got a win tell their friends. They send the link. They
send a calendar invite so a friend can hold them accountable, and so the friend
can try it too. When something works and you love it, you talk about it.

There are people besides me who sponsor and promote it: passing out fliers,
putting it up on boards, handing it to the people they mentor.

## What happens in the first ten minutes

Someone lands on the site and starts without even thinking about it. They don't
browse. They don't save it for later. They pick the thing that's true for them
today, and they finish it. They get their kitchen cleaned up and they're happy,
and the next day they spend a little more time on it because they want to.

If they're not somewhere they can act, they pick the exact time they'll do it,
and when that time comes, the steps are waiting for them.

## What we offer, and what we refuse

The checklists are free, and they always will be.

People gladly pay for help taking action: automated help that starts with the
calendar reminder and goes further, and coaching, either over the phone or
recorded, for the people who want a person in their corner.

What I refuse to do, even though it would make money, is run a subscription
that people pay for and don't use. I won't be the gym that everyone joins in
January and feels good about, that nobody uses by June, and that throws a pizza
party to keep them paying. **If they're not using it, I don't want them paying
for it.** What I want is people using it all the time and taking action.

## Dave

Dave is almost 80. He was the original life manager, and his name is honored
here *[a photo of him, and a page that tells his story, with his permission,
which he has already given]*. He doesn't have an active role. He's the person I
aspire to be like, and his concepts are the ones I'm still learning to apply.
I keep asking him questions, and his answers keep showing up in the work.

In 2019 I wrote to him, "Before you depart this world, we at least need to
write that book, it's just too good not to pass on!" And he wrote that he was
sure he could help "a lot of foolish young people not to be as stupid as I
was." This is that.

## How it runs

I spend about ten hours a week on it. The bots do most of the rest: they run
the email, watch the site, check the numbers and draft what's next. Other people
help as sponsors and promoters. The site works whether or not I'm at my desk.

## Partners

Ministries, schools and colleges, the military service academies, college
ministries, landlords I know, and the mentoring organizations I work with. They
keep coming back because every checklist helps their people actually do
something.

The service academies are a goal I'm working toward. Dave does ministry at the
Air Force Academy, and that's where we start.

## The numbers that matter

The number I watch most is **people who finish their first checklist.**

Paying customers, revenue and referrals matter too. In the first year I
guessed around $1,000 in revenue, 50 to 100 paying customers, and about 100
referrals. *[For 2029, Charles to fill in: first checklists finished per year,
paying customers, referrals.]*

## What the money does

I don't need the money. I already have a plan to give it away, and I keep
giving it away. Maybe there's a scholarship that covers coaching for people who
can't pay. Maybe the community votes on how to spend ten or twenty percent of
what comes in.

## Where faith shows up

It shows up because it's just who I am: making things that are useful to
people. Nobody has to believe anything to use a checklist.

And for the people who want more, there's a next step. Once they've learned to
act and use their time and their things well, some of them find their way into
the stewardship curriculum, and through it into churches and charities that are
using it. They see real change in their lives, and it becomes useful to the
people around them.

## Why it was worth it

Because people are telling me they got value out of it. That's what makes it
worth it.
