# Principles

These are the durable convictions behind Mr. Life Manager. They are meant to
outlast any particular product.

**Principles vs. frameworks:** a principle is what we believe and why. A
framework is the working model that makes a principle usable. Several principles
below have a full framework in [`frameworks/`](../../frameworks/); the rest are
stated here and applied throughout the content.

---

## 1. Everything has its place

*Nothing should have to be decided twice about where it lives.*

Most household disorder isn't a cleaning problem — it's an **undefined-home
problem.** An object without an assigned place has to be re-decided every time
it's set down, and re-deciding is expensive. Multiply that by a few hundred
objects and the result is a home that resists being tidy.

The principle extends far past objects. Tasks, documents, money, and
responsibilities all behave the same way: when they have no defined place, they
end up stored in your head, and your head is the least reliable container you
own.

→ Full framework: [everything-has-a-place.md](../../frameworks/everything-has-a-place.md)

---

## 2. Do it now — when "now" is actually the right answer

*Delay has a cost. So does doing everything immediately.*

Small tasks handled at the moment of noticing almost never become large tasks.
The plate rinsed now doesn't become the sink later. The email answered now
doesn't become the anxiety later.

But "do it now" taken literally is a productivity trap — it turns you into an
interrupt handler and guarantees nothing important gets finished. The real
principle is a **decision rule**: when something arrives, immediately decide
whether to do it, schedule it, delegate it, or discard it. The *deciding* is
what happens now. The *doing* might not.

→ Full framework: [do-it-now.md](../../frameworks/do-it-now.md)

---

## 3. Preventative management beats crisis management

*Small effort on a schedule beats large effort under pressure.*

Nearly every household disaster was a cheap, boring task somebody skipped:
the filter, the gutter, the oil change, the renewal, the checkup, the hard
conversation. Crises are almost always deferred maintenance with interest.

Prevention feels unrewarding precisely because it works — you never see the
crisis you avoided. That's why it has to be scheduled rather than felt.

→ Full framework: [preventative-vs-crisis.md](../../frameworks/preventative-vs-crisis.md)

## 4. Systems beat memory, and systems beat motivation

*If it depends on remembering or on feeling like it, it will fail.*

Memory and motivation are both real, both useful, and both unreliable in exactly
the conditions where you need them most — when you're tired, busy, sick, sad, or
overwhelmed.

A system is anything that produces the right behavior without requiring you to
remember or want to do it: a calendar entry, a checklist, an autopay, a hook by
the door, a recurring Sunday reset, a shared list your spouse can see.

**Practical test:** *Would this still happen on your worst week?* If the honest
answer is no, you have an intention, not a system.

Corollary: **write it down, put it on the calendar, or automate it.** Anything
held only in your head is already at risk.

---

## 5. Make the right action easy

*Design beats discipline. Reduce friction until the good habit is the lazy one.*

People do what's easy. That's not a character flaw, it's physics. So the design
question is never "how do I make myself do this?" but "how do I make this take
eight seconds instead of two minutes?"

- The hamper belongs where clothes come off, not down the hall.
- The cleaning supplies belong in the room they're used in.
- The bill belongs on autopay, not on your conscience.
- The vacuum belongs in the open, not behind three boxes.

Every step, every container to open, every decision required is friction. Remove
enough friction and behavior changes without any willpower being spent.

The inverse is equally useful: **add friction to what you want less of.**

---

## 6. Reset before things become overwhelming

*Regular small resets prevent the catastrophic big one.*

Disorder compounds. A room that's 20% out of place takes ten minutes to fix. The
same room at 80% takes a full Saturday and feels awful — so it gets postponed,
and it gets worse.

The answer is a **rhythm of resets** at different intervals:

| Reset | Duration | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Closing shift | 10 min, nightly | Kitchen, surfaces, tomorrow's setup |
| Weekly reset | 45–90 min | Laundry, bathroom, floors, fridge, calendar, money |
| Monthly reset | 1–2 hrs | Maintenance tasks, deeper clean, paperwork, subscriptions |
| Seasonal reset | Half day, 4×/year | Filters, wardrobe, deep clean, systems review |

The goal is not a spotless home. It's that **nothing ever gets far enough from
baseline to feel unrecoverable.**

## 7. Hospitality is the outcome of good stewardship

*Order is not the point. Order makes room for people.*

This is the principle that determines what kind of company this is.

A tidy home that nobody enters is a museum. The reason to run a household well
is that a home under control can be **opened** — to friends, to neighbors, to
someone who needs a meal or a place to stay, to a spontaneous "come over."

Most people don't host because hosting feels like it requires a two-day
preparation sprint. A home kept near baseline can absorb guests on short notice.
Systems produce margin; margin produces welcome.

Every product should be able to answer: *does this ultimately help someone open
their home and life to other people?*

**On the convictions underneath this.** Hospitality and stewardship are where the
founder's Christian faith enters the work — and they enter it as *reasons*, not
as requirements. Nobody has to share those convictions to use any of this;
the words carry their full ordinary meaning for any reader. Settled in
[ADR-009](../../planning/decisions.md#adr-009--faith-framing-convictions-inform-the-work-belief-is-never-required-to-use-it).

## 8. Dignity, not shame

*Nobody taught them. That's the premise, and it changes the tone of everything.*

The audience often carries quiet embarrassment about what they don't know. Some
of them are highly competent professionals who genuinely do not know how often
to wash sheets or what a P-trap is.

So the voice is: **matter-of-fact, warm, specific, and never condescending.**
No lectures. No implication that they should already know. No aesthetic
moralizing about clutter or minimalism. Just: here's how it works, here's why,
here's the shortest path.

---

## 9. Simple enough to actually keep

*A system you abandon is worth less than a cruder one you maintain.*

The failure mode of this entire category is over-engineering: the elaborate
label-maker system, the twelve-category budget, the app that requires daily
input. They're satisfying to build and impossible to sustain.

Bias toward the **smallest system that solves the problem.** Fewer categories,
fewer steps, fewer tools, paper over app when paper wins. If a system needs
maintenance of its own, it's probably too big.

---

## 10. Teach the why, then the what

*Rules without reasons don't transfer.*

"Wipe the counter" is an instruction. "Wipe the counter while the pan is still
on the stove, because dried is ten times harder than wet, and because a clear
counter is what makes tomorrow's cooking feel possible" is a transferable
principle.

Give the reasoning once, briefly, and the person can adapt the practice to a
situation you never described. That's the difference between a checklist and an
education — and it's why this is a teaching business, not a chore list.

---

## Using these principles

- **Product filter:** every product should serve at least one principle
  explicitly. Name it in the product brief.
- **Content filter:** if a piece of content contradicts #8 (dignity) or #9
  (simplicity), rewrite it.
- **Scope filter:** if an opportunity has nothing to do with practical systems,
  it's outside the business — however profitable it looks.
