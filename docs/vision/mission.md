# Mission

## Purpose of this document

To land on one mission statement that is short enough to remember, specific
enough to say no with, and honest about what the business actually does.

A good mission statement here has to pass four tests:

1. **Names the gap.** These skills were never taught — that's the whole premise.
2. **Names the method.** Systems, not motivation.
3. **Names the outcome.** An orderly, functional, hospitable life.
4. **Doesn't sound like a cleaning company or a productivity guru.**

---

## Candidates

### 1. The plain one
> **Mr. Life Manager teaches the practical systems of adulthood that people were expected to know but were never taught.**

*Strengths:* Accurate, immediately understood, states the gap plainly.
*Weaknesses:* Descriptive rather than inspiring. Slightly long. Ends on a
negative ("never taught") rather than on the outcome.

### 2. The outcome one
> **We help people build simple systems that make their homes and lives orderly, functional, and welcoming.**

*Strengths:* Ends on hospitality, which is where the brand actually points.
*Weaknesses:* Loses the "nobody taught you" hook — the thing that makes someone
feel seen. Could describe an organizing consultant.

### 3. The dignity one
> **We teach capable adults the practical skills of running a life — without shame, without lectures, and without pretending they should have already known.**

*Strengths:* Emotionally true. Directly addresses the shame that keeps people
from buying. Strong differentiator against productivity culture.
*Weaknesses:* Three "withouts" is a lot of negative space. Defines the brand by
what it isn't.

### 4. The stewardship one
> **We equip people to steward their time, money, possessions, and relationships well — starting with the home they live in.**

*Strengths:* Names all four resources. "Starting with the home" explains why
cleaning is the entry point without making it the brand. Quietly carries the
founder's convictions without alienating a general audience.
*Weaknesses:* "Steward" is a word some of the target audience won't reach for
naturally. "Equip" is a little churchy/corporate.

### 5. The short one
> **Practical systems for running an adult life.**

*Strengths:* Memorable. Fits anywhere. Could be a tagline and a mission at once.
*Weaknesses:* Too thin to make decisions with. Doesn't say who it's for or why
they need it.

### 6. The combined one
> **Mr. Life Manager gives first-time independent adults the practical systems nobody taught them — so their homes and lives are orderly, functional, and ready for the people in them.**

*Strengths:* Audience + gap + method + outcome in one sentence. Usable as a
filter: if a product idea doesn't serve first-time independent adults with a
practical system, it's out.
*Weaknesses:* Long. Locks in the beachhead audience, which may need to widen
later.

---

## Recommendation

**Adopt #6 as the working mission, with #5 as the public-facing short form.**

> **Mission (internal):** Mr. Life Manager gives first-time independent adults
> the practical systems nobody taught them — so their homes and lives are
> orderly, functional, and ready for the people in them.
>
> **Short form (external):** Practical systems for running an adult life.

### Why

- The internal version is a **decision tool**. Every product, article, and
  partnership can be tested against it: *Does this give a first-time independent
  adult a practical system?* If no, it's out of scope for now.
- "Ready for the people in them" is doing real work. It points at hospitality as
  the destination, not tidiness. That is the single thing that separates this
  from every other organizing brand.
- The short form is what goes on a homepage. Nobody memorizes a 25-word mission.

### Adjustment, 2026-09-08

[ADR-010](../../planning/decisions.md#adr-010--beachhead-keep-the-audience-change-the-payer)
separated *who the content serves* from *who is asked to pay*. The mission above
names the audience, which is still right — but it must not be read as naming the
buyer.

A version that survives that distinction:

> **Mr. Life Manager gives people the practical systems nobody taught them — so
> their homes and lives are orderly, functional, and ready for the people in
> them.**

Dropping "first-time independent adults" costs less than it looks. First
independence remains the **sharpest expression** of the need and the clearest
marketing trigger, but the need is not age-bound, and the research found the
people who *pay* are usually further along. Keep the transition as the story;
don't let it become the ceiling.

### What to revisit

- Whether the shorter version above should simply replace the recommendation,
  once copy has been written against both.
- Test whether "systems" reads as helpful or intimidating to the actual
  audience. Some people hear "system" and think "one more thing to maintain."
  Candidate substitutes to test: *routines*, *setups*, *how-to*.
