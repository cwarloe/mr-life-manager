## 1. Verdict in five lines (ranked)

1. **The pages are written for someone at home, but almost everyone who scans
   a QR code is somewhere else.** The most useful line on every audience page
   ("Not home? Pick a time and it goes on your calendar") is in small type
   under the fold. Move it into the hero and say how small the ask is: three
   taps on the next page.
2. **None of the three headlines names a problem the reader has today.**
   Career and military are nod-along lines ("Your job gets your best hours",
   "You've passed a hundred inspections"), and students is a product
   description ("Six steps. Then you're done."). Ship these instead:
   **Career:** "Too tired to deal with your place tonight? Pick the night you
   will." **Students:** "Your room's a mess and you don't know where to start.
   Start here, and stop after six steps." **Military:** "Your place isn't
   squared away yet. Get it there in six steps."
3. **Write door titles as conditions that are true while the reader is away
   from home, not moments that are only true at home.** "Dinner's ready" and
   "Move-in day" are only true in the kitchen or on one day. "You've been
   eating on the couch because the table's covered" and "New duty station,
   and your household goods haven't shown up" are true in a coffee shop.
4. **Cut the "how it works" strip ("Done means done") and "Your place, your
   call."** Long copy earns its place when the decision is big and the reader
   has objections to work through. Here the decision is free and tiny, and
   the only objections are "I'm not home" and "this will take forever." Each
   of those takes one sentence. Keep the long copy for the task pages, where
   the "Why?" buttons already hold it.
5. **Charles Fan Club:** use the Mission wording ("Pick a mission. Show up.")
   with the next real event and an "I'm in" button right under it. End every
   recap with the next date and the same button. Cut each poll to three or
   four finalists so people actually vote.

**What I'd ship first:** the hero rewrite on all three audience pages
(headline, lede and the not-home line), because it changes what every scanner
sees in the first five seconds. **What I'd cut first:** the how-it-works strip
and the homepage sub-headline, which promises a "daily rhythm" the site
doesn't currently offer.

## 2. What the evidence says

Grades: **A** = randomized field trial or meta-analysis. **B** = peer-reviewed
lab study, or large observational data. **C** = industry benchmark built from
vendor data (correlational). **D** = practitioner books, vendor case studies,
anecdote. Findings first, then my judgment, labeled.

### 2a. Long copy versus short copy

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| The direct-response tradition holds that long copy outsells short. That claim comes from split-run tests of mail-order ads and sales letters, read at home by someone holding a coupon, for products that cost money. | Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983); Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods* | D. The tests were real, but they aren't published as controlled studies, and the setting is the opposite of a QR scan. |
| How much argument helps depends on how involved the reader is. With low involvement, the number of arguments works as a shortcut ("there's a lot here, so it must be good"). With high involvement, quality decides: more strong arguments help and more weak ones hurt. | Petty & Cacioppo 1984, *J. Personality and Social Psychology* 46(1), 69–81 | B for the mechanism. It transfers weakly to a phone landing page. |
| Across 41,000+ landing pages, word count and reading time were *negatively* correlated with conversion (about −19% for both in the 2024 highlights). Pages written at roughly a 5th–7th grade reading level converted at close to twice the rate of pages written at a professional level. | [Unbounce Conversion Benchmark Report](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/) (2020–2024 editions; [methodology](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/methodology/)) | C. This is correlational and confounded, because long pages tend to sell complicated or expensive things. Unbounce itself says to test. |
| The best-known case for long pages is Crazy Egg's homepage, which [Conversion Rate Experts](https://conversion-rate-experts.com/crazy-egg-case-study/) made much longer. Crazy Egg's [own account](https://crazyegg.com/blog/high-performing-landing-pages) says the longer page beat the control by 30%, and that a later *shorter* page raised paid signups by 102.5%. Their lesson: length should match what the visitor needs to decide. | Vendor case studies | D |
| On an average page visit, people read at most about 28% of the words, and 20% is more likely. | [Nielsen 2008, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) | B− (log data, desktop era) |
| Single message-design choices, such as framing, length or wording, have small average effects on persuasion and don't reliably replicate across settings. | [O'Keefe & Hoeken 2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160/pdf) | A− (review of meta-analyses) |

**What this supports.** Charles is right that long copy can sell. The research
says when: the reader is motivated, has time, faces a decision with real cost
or risk, and each paragraph answers an objection they actually have. A QR
scanner in a coffee shop has none of those conditions. The offer is free, with
no account and no app, so there's nothing to justify. Two objections stand in
the way: "I'm not home" and "how long will this take?" Each is answered in
one sentence, and both answers are on the page already, just in the wrong
place.

**Judgment.** On the audience pages, length works against action, because
every line above the doors pushes the doors further down a phone screen. The
task pages are where long copy pays. Someone who is home and has started needs
the reason for step 4 so they don't quit at step 4, and the "Why?" buttons
already do that job. The O'Keefe and Hoeken result is a warning against
wordsmithing your way to big gains: the structure (doors, then a pick-a-time
step) matters more than any one headline. That is also why section 7 tests
the big steps rather than commas.

### 2b. Specificity

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| When customer-service employees used more concrete language, customers were more satisfied, more willing to buy, and bought more, in field data from more than 1,000 real conversations and in experiments. The mechanism was that customers felt *listened to*. | Packard & Berger 2021, *J. Consumer Research* 47(5), 787–806, [doi:10.1093/jcr/ucaa038](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaa038) | A−/B (field plus lab; the setting is a service conversation, not an ad) |
| Writing down a date *and a time* raised flu-shot uptake significantly. A date alone did not. | Milkman et al. 2011, *PNAS* (cited in [deferred-win.md](deferred-win.md)) | A |
| Self-referencing (getting the reader to relate the message to their own life) raises persuasion when the arguments are sound. | Burnkrant & Unnava 1995, *J. Consumer Research* | B |

**Judgment.** The Packard and Berger mechanism, "they understand my
situation," explains why a door titled "You've been eating on the couch
because the table's covered" should beat "Dinner's ready. There's nowhere to
eat." The first describes the reader's evening. Real times ("tonight at 7,"
"Saturday at 10") belong on the page because they match the defaults the
picker actually offers.

### 2c. Problem-first versus benefit-first headlines

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Framing a message as a loss ("what you lose if you don't") versus a gain makes little difference overall. Gain frames were slightly more persuasive for prevention behaviors, and loss frames had a tiny edge (r ≈ −.04) for detection behaviors. | O'Keefe & Jensen 2006, 2007 (*J. Health Communication*), 2009 (*J. Communication*) | A (meta-analyses) |

**What this supports.** "Problem-first" doesn't work because pain is more
persuasive than gain. As a framing effect, that's close to zero. It works
because it sorts readers: someone recognizes their own situation in a door
title and taps it (self-referencing, and the Packard and Berger
"listened to" effect).

**Judgment.** Name the problem in the headline and door title, which is what
gets the tap. Put the end state (the benefit) in the door description, which
is what makes the tap feel worth it. Keep the problem specific enough to sort
people, and phrase it without blame: describe the situation, not the person.

### 2d. Commitment and implementation intentions (the "pick a time" step)

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| About half of intentions never become action. | Sheeran & Webb 2016 ([deferred-win.md](deferred-win.md)) | A |
| If-then plans that state when and where ("implementation intentions") had a medium-to-large effect across 94 studies (d = 0.65). | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 | A (later analyses suggest smaller effects in real-world behavior change) |
| Phone calls that asked voters *when* they'd vote, *where* they'd be coming from and *what they'd be doing beforehand* raised turnout 4.1 points among people reached. In single-voter households the lift was 9.1 points. Plain encouragement, and asking whether they intended to vote, did nothing significant. | [Nickerson & Rogers 2010, *Psychological Science*](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/nickerson20and20rogers_FINAL.pdf) | A |
| Making a specific plan stopped an unfinished goal from intruding on other work. | Masicampo & Baumeister 2011 | B |
| Reminders framed as "this is reserved for you" did best among 19 messages tested. | Milkman et al. 2021, *PNAS* | A |
| People are more likely to start goals right after "fresh start" landmarks: a new week, month or semester. | Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014 | A− |

**What this supports.** The pick-a-time mechanism is the best-supported part
of the whole funnel, and the copy around it is already good. "'Later' isn't a
time." and "That's decided." are exactly right. The Nickerson and Rogers
finding says the plan works better with a **first action** attached ("when I
get home at 7, I'll start with the sink"). Their single-voter finding suggests
it matters most for people who live alone, which describes much of the career
audience and many junior officers.

**Judgment.** The calendar event currently uses the page's `data-task` title
("Get the house livable again"). It would work harder if it named the first
step: "Step 1 of 6: Reset the kitchen. Steps are ready." That is a small
change in `entry.js`. Students and military readers won't say "house," so the
`data-task` titles should say "place." The "Send it to someone who'll check on
you" button fits Dave's own rule ("Have someone check you"), but the published
evidence on social accountability is mixed, so treat it as judgment and watch
the `shared-with-checker` count.

### 2e. Audience-specific evidence

**Gen Z / students.** The evidence is thin, and most of it comes from
industry surveys about "authenticity" and peer trust, so I grade it D and
lean on judgment. **Don't cite the "eight-second attention span" figure.**
It traces to a 2015 Microsoft Canada marketing report and is not a valid
measurement. What's defensible: plain, specific, unpolished language reads as
honest, and a friend's link is the most trusted channel, which makes the link
preview (`<title>` and meta description, injected as `og:title` and
`og:description` by `build_site.py`) part of the copy. Fresh-start timing
(Dai et al.) favors the start of a semester, the first week after a break,
and Sunday or Monday fliers.

**Early-career professionals.** From the repo's own research
([high-intensity-professionals.md](high-intensity-professionals.md)), coming
home to piled-up chores is a recognized stress point for business travelers
(Medium), and preventive coping makes travel go better (High for the finding).
That supports the career page's existing travel note, which *is* an act-now
line: schedule the evening you land, now.

**Military.**
- Army writing standards (AR 25-50) require the bottom line up front and
  active voice ([BLUF overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication))).
  Grade B for the standard. **Judgment:** a reader trained this way will
  reward a headline that states the task and the result plainly, and may read
  heavy jargon ("Execute now") as a civilian wearing a costume. Use the plain
  terms they actually use, like "duty station," "household goods" and
  "squared away," and skip the cosplay.
- **Junior enlisted service members mostly live in barracks or dorms, which
  are still inspected.** Single members in the lowest grades are generally
  required to live in unaccompanied housing. The rules vary by service: Air
  Force E-1 to E-3 and E-4s under three years; Navy E-1 to E-3 and E-4s under
  four years; installation pages cite E-5 and below for Army and Marines
  ([Military OneSource](https://militaryonesource.mil/moving-pcs/housing/living-in-the-military-barracks),
  [GAO-03-257R](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-03-257r)). Grade B− (rules
  change; verify with someone in uniform). **This makes the current military
  headline untrue for a large part of the stated audience.** "Nobody
  inspecting it but you" (the page's meta description) describes an officer
  or an NCO living off base, not an E-3 in the barracks. See section 4.
- They move every two to three years (repo research). **Judgment, from
  common military experience rather than a study:** the most specific pain at
  a new station is the gap before household goods arrive, and the most
  common leftover is a garage or spare room of unopened boxes from the last
  move. Both map onto live checklists (first-night and one-room).

## 4. Headline options and recommended page copy

The same rules hold throughout. The headline names a problem the reader has
today, in their words. The lede says what happens when they tap. One line
removes "I'm not home." Every claim matches the live checklists: underwater
about 90 minutes, guests about an hour, dinner about 10 minutes, first-night
about 90 minutes, one-room about 3 hours. "Three taps" is literal: **Not
right now → Tonight → Add to my calendar.**

### 4a. Career (`/career`)

**Options, ranked**

1. **Kicker:** Long hours · travel · on call
   **H1:** Too tired to deal with your place tonight? *Pick the night you will.*
   **Lede:** Tap the one that's true. Each one is six steps on your phone, with no account and no app.
   *Why it gets the scan-to-tap-to-start:* it names the reader's real state at
   the moment they scan (tired, not home), turns not acting now into the
   action (picking a night), and makes the ask small. It's the closest match
   to the research on what works.
2. **H1:** Work took the week. *Get your place back in one evening.*
   **Lede:** Tap the one that's true. You get six steps on your phone, and none of them takes all night.
   *Why:* problem first, with an honest, bounded promise (every door on this
   page is 90 minutes or less). It works better for start-now readers, such
   as a friend's text read at home.
3. **H1:** The trip isn't the problem. *Coming back to a mess is.*
   **Lede:** Pick the evening you land, now. That night your calendar opens six steps that get the place back.
   *Why:* backed by the travel research, and the lede turns the insight
   straight into an action. Use it only on placements near travelers
   (airport-adjacent coffee shops, consulting offices); it narrows the
   audience.
4. **H1:** You get home late, and the place is a mess. *Ninety minutes gets it livable again.*
   **Lede:** Six steps on your phone: dishes, one room, the bed, dirty clothes and the mail. Nothing else.
   *Why:* very concrete, but it sells one door, which is fine if the
   numbers show that door dominates.
5. Current: "Your job gets your best hours. Here's what to do with what's left." Readers agree and move on. Retire it.

- **Meta description:** Pick the night you'll deal with it. Six steps on your phone, no account, and it goes on your calendar in three taps.

## 6. Charles Fan Club

**Its job is different, and the copy should be judged by it.** It's a small,
invitation-only club of friends: kids, teens and the parents who RSVP for
them. Nobody needs persuading that a problem exists. The job is **getting
members to come to the next event and to vote in polls.** The equivalent of
the act-now filter here: *does this line get a member to tap "I'm in," add the
date to their calendar, or vote?* A warm line about friendship can still earn
its place, because belonging is part of what brings people back. A line about
following along from a distance doesn't.

**Evidence that transfers (section 2d):** plan-making raised turnout when it
asked when, where and what you'd be doing beforehand (Nickerson & Rogers,
grade A). Descriptive norms ("most people like you do X") raise compliance
when they're true (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008, hotel towel reuse,
*J. Consumer Research*; grade A−). Reminders framed as reserved for you work
best (Milkman et al. 2021). Choice sets that are complex, with no obvious
favorite, can make people put off choosing (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman
2015 meta-analysis, *J. Consumer Psychology*; grade A for the moderators,
while the average effect is small).

### Critique of the four wording sets (`public/docs/index.html`)

| Set | Lines | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Mission** | "Pick a mission. Show up." · "I'm in" · "Mission list" · "Stay in the loop." · "Get your crew card." | **The best fit for the job.** The headline is two actions in the right order. "I'm in" is a first-person commitment, which beats "RSVP." Replace "Stay in the loop" (passive) with "Put every mission on your calendar." "Details post as soon as they're confirmed" is fine. |
| **Story** (current homepage) | "The crew shows up." · "Follow the story as it happens." · "Don't miss an issue." · "To be continued…" | "The crew shows up" is a descriptive norm, and it's a good line *if it's true*: lean on it once attendance is steady. "Follow the story as it happens" and "To be continued…" invite people to watch, not to come. Keep "Issue #1" as decoration; it's charming and costs nothing. |
| **Sports** | "Show up. Do work. Have fun." · "Count me in" · "Never miss a game." · "Collect them all" | "Count me in" is good. "Do work" is slang that can read as chores to kids and as odd to parents. **"Never miss a game" conflicts with the club's own rule** ("no scoreboards, nobody keeps score of who shows up"). It pressures attendance. Cut it. |
| **Plain** | "For friendship. For service." · "Friendship. Service. Showing up." · "RSVP" | It's the club's values and its legacy tagline. Keep it **as the tagline under the logo**, not as the headline. "RSVP" is the weakest button label of the four. |

### Homepage (`public/index.html`)

- **H1 "The crew shows up." with "See the calendar" and "Vote in the poll":**
  the two buttons are the right two actions. The hero should **show the next
  confirmed event**, with its date and an "I'm in" button, instead of
  pointing at the calendar. That takes one tap instead of two, and the date
  is the reason to act.
- **"Fan Club serves. Run Club runs. Bible Club digs in."** Keep it. It's
  short and specific.
- **"How it works: Clubs are by invitation / Members sign in / Show up":**
  members already know this, and visitors who aren't members can't act on
  it. It belongs on the login page and the public proposal page.
- **"Help pick our look… Pick the one that feels like us, then vote.":** this
  is good, because it ends on the action. Add a real closing date if there is
  one ("Voting closes Sunday"). Make up a deadline only if Charles will
  actually close the poll then.

### Event pages (`public/docs/events/*`)

- **"The mission, short: We box food at the food bank, side by side with
  friends. Come ready to work, leave knowing it mattered."** Keep it. It's
  concrete, and the last clause is the reason kids come back.
- **Recaps end with "See you next month."** Rewrite every recap ending as
  **"Next one: [day, date]. Tap I'm in."** with the button. A member reading
  the recap is at their most enthusiastic, so that's the moment to ask (the
  same idea as fresh starts).
- **After "I'm in," add a plan-making prompt:** "Who's driving, and what time
  are you leaving?" or, for parents, "Add it to your calendar" plus "A parent
  is staying to help." This is the Nickerson and Rogers result applied
  directly. The ticket design's "A parent is staying to help" checkbox is
  already halfway there.
- **"By the numbers… about 12 friends showed up"** (sample). Totals for the
  whole crew, with no per-person counts, are right. They work as a true
  descriptive norm for the next one.
- **Event-design choice:** judged only on this job, **Ticket and Prints** has
  the strongest action copy. The tear-off stub per kid ("Coming / Can't") is
  the clearest one-tap commitment in the set. The Case File's blacked-out
  lines are fun, but they put "you can't see this" first for members who are
  signed out.

### Polls

- **"Design round 2: which website look is your favorite?" has 16 options**,
  and the instructions say "Open each preview, then pick your favorite."
  That's a complex set with no obvious favorite, plus homework before
  voting, which are the conditions where people put off choosing. **Cut it
  to three or four finalists, each with a thumbnail in the poll itself**, and
  say "It takes one tap."
- "Thanks, your vote is saved." and "Update your vote": keep both.

### Recommended headline options (Fan Club), ranked

1. **"Pick a mission. Show up."** with the next event card and **"I'm in"** directly under it. Tagline under the logo: *For friendship. For service.*
2. **"Next up: [Food bank day], [Sat, Nov 21]. Are you in?"**, a headline filled in from the calendar. It's the strongest for showing up, but it needs a small build.
3. **"The crew shows up."** Use it once attendance makes it plainly true. It's a norm, not a request, so pair it with the next date.
4. **"Friendship. Service. Showing up."** Plain and warm, with no call to act. Best in the footer or on the card back.
5. Cut: "Show up. Do work. Have fun." and "Never miss a game."

**Kid view:** "Ask a parent to RSVP" is correct, because the kid's real
action is asking. Make it specific: "Ask your parent to tap **I'm in** for
Tuesday's run."

## 7. What to test with real QR traffic

**Mechanics already in place.** Every QR code carries `?from=qr-<place>`.
`audience.js` passes `from` to the checklist. Umami records the checklist
pageview (that's the door tap), then `start-now`, `not-right-now`,
`time-picked` (with the choice), `calendar-added` (ics or google),
`shared-with-checker`, `came-back`, `started-from-reminder`, `finished`,
`rescheduled` and `let-go`, all filterable by `from`.

**Volume reality.** Detecting a difference between 10% and 20% on any step
needs roughly **200 visits per variant** (two-sided, α = .05, 80% power).
Expect a few dozen scans per flier per month, so most headline tests won't
reach significance this term. That's why the tests below go after **large
differences and big funnel steps**, and why the door split is the main
learning.

**Tag scheme.** `qr-<place>-<variant>`, such as `qr-bluecup-a` and
`qr-bluecup-b`, or `qr-commons-tent-a`. These fit the `[a-z0-9-]{1,40}`
rule. Rotate variants at the **same placement in alternating weeks**. Don't
put two variants side by side, because position will decide the result.

**Tests, in order**

1. **Which problems each audience actually has (no variants needed).** By
   audience and placement, record the share of door taps for each door. This
   answers the tracker's main question, whether audiences need different
   checklists, faster than any headline test. *Decision rule:* a door with
   under 10% of taps after about 100 taps gets replaced. A door with over
   40% gets its own flier headline.
2. **Flier headline A/B per audience (scans per week).** Run the
   section-5 flier against a version that uses the current page headline.
   Measure visits per week for each tag. This is the only way to test what
   gets the scan. It needs no site change.
3. **"Not right now" against "Not home yet? Pick a time."** on the task
   pages. Measure (`time-picked` + `start-now`) ÷ checklist visits for
   `from=qr-*` traffic. **This is probably the highest-leverage single copy
   change**, because nearly every QR reader goes through it. It needs a small
   switch in `entry.js` keyed to a `v=` parameter.
4. **Fork above the frame for QR traffic.** Measure the same ratio as test 3.
   Run it after test 3, not at the same time.
5. **Page headline A/B** (section 4 option 1 against option 2 for each
   audience). Measure door taps ÷ landing visits. This needs `audience.js` to
   swap the H1 and lede by `v=`, and fliers that carry `v=a` or `v=b`.
   Expect it to need a full term of traffic.
6. **Default time picks.** Read the `time-picked` choice distribution and
   `came-back` ÷ `time-picked` by choice. If "Saturday morning" picks come
   back at twice the rate of "Tonight," lead with Saturday. For one-room,
   lead with Saturday regardless.
7. **Calendar title naming step 1** against the current `data-task` title.
   Measure `started-from-reminder` ÷ `calendar-added`. This is the
   implementation-intention test. Its effect shows up only in completions,
   so it's slow, but it's the purest test of the evidence in section 2d.

**Read the whole funnel, not one number:** visits → door tap → (start now or
time picked) → calendar added → came back → started from reminder →
finished. Per the deferred-win research, expect about half of scheduled
readers not to come back. That's the base rate, not a failure.

**Cheap qualitative check before printing:** a five-second test with five
people per audience. Show the flier for five seconds, take it away, and ask
"What is this for, and what would you do?" Use Charles's coworkers for
career, Dave's Academy contacts (within what his volunteer role allows) for
military, and a campus-ministry referrer's students for students. If two of
five can't say "scan it and pick the thing that's true," rewrite before
printing.

**Fan Club** (too small for A/B): measure the "I'm in" rate per event and
votes per poll before and after (a) the next-event card in the hero, (b)
recap endings that link the next date, and (c) cutting polls to four options.
Then ask three parents what made them RSVP.
