# Can people finish the five entry checklists?

Research and task analysis, 2026-10-28. Reviewed public copy at commit
`baef666022ede50828bd4f4b59db66714122d063`, with an independent agent review.

## Finding

Do not mark all five as validated. Dinner has the strongest task design;
first-night and guests are plausible under unstated conditions; underwater
and one-room need changes before their advertised times are defensible.
These are design judgments, not measured completion rates or diagnoses.
No participant sessions were conducted for this audit.

Charles's constraint applies: keep the full useful outcome. Do not redefine
finishing as doing the first one or two steps. If the full task needs more
time, say so. Six headings are a navigation choice, not a cognitive capacity
limit: several headings contain many actions and decisions.

## Evidence and its limits

- **Stress:** Shields, Sazma & Yonelinas (2016), [meta-analysis of acute stress
  and executive functions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003767/),
  found average impairments in working memory and cognitive flexibility, with
  important moderators and mixed inhibition effects. Design inference: reduce
  remembered substeps and unexpected decisions for a stressed reader. This
  does not measure household-task performance or establish an ADHD treatment.
- **External support:** Risko & Gilbert (2016), [Cognitive
  Offloading](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/), reviews using actions
  and external resources to change cognitive demands. Design inference: keep
  current instructions visible and use labeled containers and reminders.
  A reminder alone does not ensure a person can perform the task.
- **Instruction specificity:** Eiriksdottir & Catrambone (2011), [review of
  procedural instructions, principles, and examples](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720811419154),
  distinguishes initial performance from learning and transfer. Concrete
  instructions are a better fit for immediate execution than requiring the
  novice to infer the method. Our one-step display and optional explanations
  fit that direction; the review did not test this interface.
- **Starting plans:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), [implementation-intention
  meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1), supports linking
  a situation to a specific action. Our calendar plus task/location is a
  reasonable application. It is not evidence that choosing an exact clock
  time guarantees completion or eliminates cognitive work.
- **Time estimates:** Wiese, Buehler & Griffin (2016), [four experiments on
  planning direction and completion-time predictions](https://sjdm.org/~baron/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html),
  distinguishes work time from completion time, which also includes delays
  and competing demands. Application: account for finding supplies, setup,
  reading, transitions, interruptions, cleanup, and travel. Their experiments
  do not supply a correction multiplier for our checklists.
- **Breaks:** Albulescu et al. (2022), [micro-break systematic review and
  meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460), found benefits
  for vigor and fatigue, but no significant overall performance benefit.
  Breaks are reasonable; this does not validate precisely 12 minutes working,
  two minutes resting, or five seconds deciding about possessions.

These sources support design choices. None establishes the probability that
our audience finishes these particular tasks in these particular homes.

## Route assessment

| Checklist | Current timing | Assessment | Main obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|
| First night | About 60 minutes; badges total 65 | Conditional; timing unverified | Searching for essentials, locating unfamiliar controls, testing every fixture, and reporting faults |
| Guests | About 35 minutes; badges total 34 | Conditional; little buffer | A full sink without a dishwasher, cleaning supplies, and bathroom product contact times |
| Dinner | No finish-time promise; calendar sets aside 10 minutes to start | Strongest candidate for a pilot | Basket capacity and unnecessary trips to return items |
| Mess again / underwater | About 40 minutes; badges total 41 | Needs revision | Unbounded kitchen and mail backlog before several room transitions |
| Unused room / one-room | About 120 minutes; badges total 125 | Needs revision | Unbounded zone, repeated disposal decisions, supplies and transport, inconsistent donation finish line |

The small rounding differences are not the central problem. The estimates
mostly leave no allowance for variability. Do not manufacture replacement
time ranges from this desk review and label them research findings.

### First night

1. Photos: 15 minutes assumes a small accessible place and working phone;
   every room, appliance interior and existing mark grows with property size.
2. Bed: 10 minutes assumes an assembled bed and accessible bedding. The
   instruction to pack Box Zero in a personal car arrives after moving in.
3. Bathroom: 10 minutes assumes the listed essentials are already available.
4. Controls: 10 minutes assumes controls are identifiable and accessible;
   a renter may need building management to identify a shared shutoff.
5. Checks and reports: 10 minutes hides testing multiple fixtures, reaching
   alarms safely, diagnosing an unexpected result, and writing a report.
6. Surface and seat: 10 minutes assumes furniture exists and food is available;
   preparing or acquiring a meal is not budgeted.

Proposed correction: state prerequisites before Start, make the pack-a-box
advice prospective, provide a short fault-report example, and include a clear
path for recording inaccessible controls and requesting their location. Keep
all six outcomes; do not equate a request with a completed physical check.
The deposit guarantees in the explanatory copy also exceed what photos prove.

### Guests

1. Trash/window: supply and airflow assumptions are unstated.
2. Sink: five minutes is plausible for a small load into an available
   dishwasher, not an arbitrary pile of handwashing or stuck-on pans.
3. Basket: ten minutes assumes the sitting area fits in one container and
   does not require decisions about storage or heavy objects.
4. Bathroom: eight minutes includes toilet, sink, mirror, floor, towel and
   trash. Product instructions can add waiting and tool changes.
5. Floors: five minutes assumes tools are ready and the walking route clear.
6. Exit/lighting: three minutes assumes a nearby trash destination and lamps.

Proposed correction: show supplies and suitable starting conditions up front;
use label-directed cleaning instructions, a tool fallback, and a time estimate
that includes setup. If a substantial dish backlog exists, the current full
outcome needs longer; do not silently count unfinished dishes as an empty sink.
Remove language suggesting guests will inspect or judge them; it adds pressure
without adding an action. This is a design concern, not a measured effect here.

### Dinner

1. Obvious trash gives a low-decision start.
2. Moving dishes explicitly excludes washing and directs the person back.
3. Returning known-home items has an explicit one-trip boundary.
4. The basket removes the need to decide new homes before eating.
5. Wiping has an observable result.
6. Seats and place settings complete the promised use, not just appearance.

Proposed correction: clarify that the basket must hold the remaining items,
use another available container if necessary, and keep the placement out of
walkways. No storage purchases. Preserve the distinction between a calendar
starting block and a finish-time claim. Pilot this first.

### Mess again

1. Kitchen: 15 minutes hides dishes, counters, trash and possibly stovetop.
   Neither number of dishes nor degree of soil is bounded.
2. Basket: 10 minutes assumes all loose clutter fits and can be carried.
3. Bed: four minutes is plausible only for rearranging existing bedding.
4. Clothes: five minutes assumes a reachable hamper with space. An 8 PM rule
   adds an arbitrary clock constraint; the actual task is gathering clothes.
5. Mail: five minutes could cover a few envelopes, not weeks of unknown mail.
   Bills and due dates switch the person from physical work to consequential
   reading and decisions near the end of an already tiring task.
6. Tomorrow: choosing a next action and time is useful, but not zero effort.

Proposed correction: preserve the complete reset and state the workload it
covers. If the intended audience includes a substantial backlog, enlarge the
time allowance rather than promising the same outcome in 40 minutes. Give a
concrete mail-sorting procedure and remove absolute claims about effort and
recurrence. Gather all tools before the clock starts, or include that time.

### Unused room

1. Prep: 15 minutes includes donation-location research, supplies and an empty
   vehicle. These may be real blockers, not excuses. A car is not universal.
2. Sort: three 12-minute sprints with two two-minute breaks total 40 minutes;
   the badge says 45. Neither number establishes a volume of sortable items.
3. Decisions: the five-second rule runs inside sorting, so it is not a
   separate timed block. It pressures irreversible decisions, and the one-year
   test misses seasonal, emergency, legal, borrowed and sentimental items.
4. Exit: 30 minutes depends on weight, stairs, distance, transport and drop-off
   hours. The introduction promises donations gone; the body counts a loaded
   trunk plus tomorrow's reminder as finished. Those are different outcomes.
5. Storage: 25 minutes assumes shelves, containers, labels and known homes;
   insisting on clear bins introduces a possible shopping trip.
6. Sweep: ten minutes assumes safely clear floor and available tools.

Proposed correction: define a finishable zone by its contents and intended
use before starting, establish supplies and exit arrangements, and replace
the disposal speed rule with supported categories and protected exceptions.
Use existing labeled containers. Choose a single truthful donation outcome
and use it throughout. Keep the full selected zone as the task; do not turn
one-room into an incomplete sort that leaves new piles behind.

## What a meaningful test would be

Run an exploratory pilot before claiming reliable timing. Start with dinner;
test guests and first-night next, and revise the other two before recruitment.
Do not recruit or contact people automatically from this document.

Use about ten first-time users per route as an initial product-learning round,
not a statistically representative sample. Include ordinary homes, fatigue,
different dish/storage backlogs, and people who report difficulty starting or
staying on household tasks. Do not infer diagnoses. Record starting conditions
so success among well-equipped participants is not generalized to everyone.

Give each person the page without coaching. Time from opening it to the
observable finish line, including reading, supply searches, interruptions,
and cleanup; also record active work separately. Observe quietly or review
voluntarily shared notes afterward: continuous think-aloud adds work and
can distort the timing. Note intervention, skipped actions, outside help,
confusion, missing supplies, decision stalls and unfinished piles.

Before testing, write down each route's full outcome from its page. Count
completion only when all required actions/outcomes are satisfied; a click on
Done is a self-report, not observation of a completed room. For first-night,
record faults reported separately from faults repaired. For dinner, food
preparation and later basket sorting are explicitly outside the task.

Record perceived mental effort on a simple 1–7 scale, fatigue before/after,
and whether the result felt worth the effort. These are exploratory measures,
not a validated cognitive-workload instrument. Record actual pauses rather
than treating a pause or reschedule as personal failure.

An initial product gate could be at least eight of ten finishing the full task
unassisted within the published allowance, with no serious instruction error
and no recurring blocker. This is a proposed team decision, not a scientific
standard or proof of an 80% population completion rate. Report the actual
counts, time distribution, effort ratings and starting conditions; include
non-completers and revise/retest recurring problems. Dinner's ten-minute
starting appointment is not a published finish allowance: measure its duration
first and decide what, if anything, to promise afterward.

Existing start/end/calendar analytics can identify funnel drop-off. They
cannot determine whether the physical outcome happened, why a person stopped,
or how hard the task felt. No extra analytics or participant data collection
was added by this audit.
