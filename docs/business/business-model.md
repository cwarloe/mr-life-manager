# Business Model

An exploration of how Mr. Life Manager could make money. Nothing here is
decided. The goal is to understand each model's economics, effort, and fit
before committing.

**Assumption to keep visible:** this starts as a solo, part-time venture with no
outside capital. Models that require a team, inventory, or scheduled availability
are structurally disadvantaged early on, however attractive their margins.

---

## The shape of the business

Three plausible shapes, and they lead to very different companies:

| Shape | Core engine | Ceiling | Founder time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Digital products** | Write once, sell many | High, slow to build | Front-loaded |
| **B. Services** | Sell expertise by the hour | Capped by hours | Continuous |
| **C. Licensing / B2B2C** | Institutions buy for their people | High, lumpy | Sales-heavy |

**Working hypothesis:** start with **A**, use **B** sparingly for learning and
early cash, and pursue **C** deliberately once there is a product worth
licensing. C is likely the largest long-term opportunity and the worst possible
starting point.

---

## Model 1 — Ebooks and digital guides

**What it is.** PDF/EPUB guides sold direct. The flagship: *First Place: The
Practical Guide to Running Your First Home.*

- **Economics:** ~100% gross margin. Price $19–$49.
- **Effort:** High up front (writing, editing, design), near-zero to fulfill.
- **Pros:** Fully asynchronous. Proves the content. Becomes the source material
  for every other product on this list. Easy to bundle, excerpt, and license.
- **Cons:** Ebooks are a commoditized, low-trust category. Discovery is the whole
  game. One-time revenue with no retention.
- **Risk:** Writing a 200-page book nobody wants. Mitigate by selling a short
  version, or presales, *before* writing the long one.
- **Verdict:** **Core of the MVP.** Highest fit for a solo founder.

## Model 2 — Printable guides and checklists

**What it is.** Single-purpose printables: first apartment checklist, cleaning
supply list, chore agreement, maintenance calendar, move-out checklist.

- **Economics:** ~100% margin. $0 (lead magnet) to $12.
- **Effort:** Low per item. Design quality matters more than length.
- **Pros:** Fast to produce and test — the cheapest way to learn what people
  actually want. Ideal lead magnets. Very shareable. Natural bundles.
- **Cons:** Low price point; needs volume. Easy for anyone to copy.
- **Verdict:** **Yes — as the free tier and as fast validation.** Don't expect
  meaningful direct revenue.

## Model 3 — Digital toolkits and templates

**What it is.** Bundled, structured systems: a household operating agreement, a
paperwork/document system, a maintenance calendar with reminders, a starting-over
kit. Delivered as PDFs plus Notion/Google Sheets/Calendar templates.

- **Economics:** ~100% margin. $29–$99.
- **Effort:** Medium; must actually work, which requires testing.
- **Pros:** Higher perceived value than a PDF because it's a *system*, not
  reading. Directly on-brand — this is what "practical systems" literally means.
  Excellent bundle anchor.
- **Cons:** Support burden if templates break. Platform dependency (if Notion
  changes, your product changes).
- **Verdict:** **Strong mid-tier.** Probably the most differentiated digital
  product available to this brand.

## Model 4 — Video courses

**What it is.** Structured multi-module courses: *First Place*, *30-Day Life
Reset*, *Hosting Without Panic*.

- **Economics:** High margin minus platform fees (Teachable/Podia/Kajabi,
  $0–$199/mo). $99–$299.
- **Effort:** **High** — scripting, filming, editing, hosting, updating.
- **Pros:** Much higher price point. Demonstration beats description for physical
  tasks. Good fit for institutional licensing later.
- **Cons:** Very expensive to produce for an unvalidated audience. Notoriously
  low completion rates. Aging video is costly to re-shoot.
- **Verdict:** **Not for MVP.** Revisit after a text product sells. Consider a
  low-production cohort/live version first — same price, a fraction of the cost.

## Model 5 — Membership / subscription

**What it is.** Monthly access to new content, seasonal checklists, a community,
and periodic Q&A. $9–$29/mo.

- **Economics:** Recurring revenue — the most valuable kind — but requires
  continuous production.
- **Effort:** **Perpetual.** The content treadmill never stops.
- **Pros:** Predictable revenue; deep customer relationship; strong feedback loop.
- **Cons:** Churn is brutal in self-improvement categories. Worse, it fights the
  brand: the promise is *you'll need us less over time*. A membership wants the
  opposite. And there's a real risk of building a community you must then host
  forever.
- **Verdict:** **Not for MVP, and possibly never in this form.** If pursued, frame
  it seasonally ("the rhythm of the year") rather than as a content firehose.

## Model 6 — Coaching (1:1 or group)

**What it is.** Paid sessions helping someone build their systems. $75–$200/hr,
or packages at $400–$1,500.

- **Economics:** High rate, zero leverage. Revenue stops when you stop.
- **Effort:** Continuous, and requires scheduled availability.
- **Pros:** **The single best research tool available.** Ten coaching clients
  will teach more about the real problems than a hundred survey responses. Cash
  arrives immediately. Produces testimonials and case studies.
- **Cons:** Doesn't scale. Can quietly become the whole business and strangle
  product development.
- **Verdict:** **Yes — deliberately and in limited quantity.** Sell a handful of
  slots early, treat them as paid customer research, and cap them.

## Model 7 — Household setup consultation

**What it is.** In-person (or video) setup of an actual home: layout, storage
design, supply purchasing, systems installation, maintenance calendar. $500–$2,500
per engagement.

- **Economics:** Highest per-engagement revenue. Possibly travel and materials.
- **Pros:** Premium anchor for the whole ladder. Extremely high perceived value.
  Great content and proof source.
- **Cons:** Geographically constrained. Time-intensive. Blurs into
  professional-organizer territory, which is a **different and crowded
  business** — and one that competes with the brand's own positioning.
- **Verdict:** **Offer sparingly, position carefully.** Useful as the top rung of
  the ladder and as a signal of seriousness, even at low volume. Do not let it
  become the identity.

## Model 8 — Affiliate revenue

**What it is.** Commission on recommended products — cleaning supplies, storage,
tools, kitchen basics, small appliances. Amazon Associates (1–4%) or direct
brand programs (5–20%).

- **Economics:** Passive but small at low traffic. Meaningful only at scale.
- **Pros:** Genuinely additive — this audience *is* buying starter supplies, and
  a curated "actually buy these" list is real value, not a bolt-on.
- **Cons:** **Serious integrity risk.** The moment recommendations are shaped by
  commission, trust is gone and the brand's core promise breaks.
- **Guardrails if pursued:** recommend only what would be recommended anyway;
  disclose clearly and prominently; always include a no-affiliate option and a
  cheapest-acceptable option; never let a commission change a ranking.
- **Verdict:** **Yes, later, with strict rules.** Not a strategy — a byproduct of
  content that already exists.

## Model 9 — Licensing to institutions

**What it is.** Selling the right to use Mr. Life Manager curriculum to
organizations that serve people in transition:

- **Colleges and universities** — housing/residence life, first-year experience,
  RA training, off-campus student services, senior transition programs
- **Churches and ministries** — young adult programs, premarital counseling,
  college ministries, benevolence and support ministries
- **Military** — transition assistance programs, first-duty-station onboarding,
  family readiness groups
- **Employers** — early-career onboarding and relocation support
- **Nonprofits** — foster care aging-out programs, reentry programs, refugee
  resettlement, recovery and transitional housing
- **Property managers** — new-tenant welcome kits (strong incentive alignment:
  tenants who maintain units cost less)

**Economics.** Per-seat ($5–$25/person), site license ($1,000–$10,000/yr), or
train-the-trainer packages. Long sales cycles, budget seasons, procurement.

- **Pros:** **Largest revenue potential and the strongest mission fit.** Solves
  the distribution problem for exactly the personas who can't pay individually
  (Maya, Ray). Contracts renew. Testimonials are institutional and durable.
- **Cons:** Slow. Requires a finished, credible, defensible product plus proof.
  Procurement, insurance, accessibility, and sometimes accreditation requirements.
  Consultative selling — a different skill from writing.
- **Verdict:** **The long game, and probably the real business.** Absolutely not
  the MVP. Every early product should nonetheless be built so that it *could* be
  licensed: modular, standalone, brandable, printable.

## Comparison

| Model | Margin | Effort to launch | Scalability | MVP fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ebooks | Very high | High | High | ★★★★★ |
| Printables/checklists | Very high | Low | High | ★★★★★ |
| Toolkits/templates | Very high | Medium | High | ★★★★☆ |
| Courses | High | Very high | High | ★★☆☆☆ |
| Membership | High | Very high (ongoing) | Medium | ★☆☆☆☆ |
| Coaching | High | Low | None | ★★★☆☆ (as research) |
| Setup consultation | High | Medium | None | ★★☆☆☆ |
| Affiliate | Pure margin | Low | High | ★★☆☆☆ (needs traffic) |
| Licensing | High | Very high | Very high | ★☆☆☆☆ (later, big) |

## Recommended sequence

1. **Free checklist** → prove the audience exists and build a list.
2. **One paid digital product** ($29–$49) → prove people will pay.
3. **5–10 coaching clients** → learn what's actually broken, get testimonials,
   generate cash.
4. **Toolkits and a product family** → increase revenue per customer.
5. **A course**, only if the text products sell.
6. **Licensing**, once there's a proven, modular, credible curriculum.

The first three steps cost almost nothing but time, and each one produces the
information needed for the next.

## Open questions

- Is the audience reachable at acceptable cost, or is distribution the real
  constraint? (Almost certainly the latter — plan accordingly.)
- Does the "Mr. Life Manager" persona require a visible face? Does that face have
  to be the founder?
- Does the Christian stewardship framing get stated openly, held implicitly, or
  split into two product lines for different channels? **This is a foundational
  decision** — it shapes voice, channel, and partnerships.
- Should `products/` and business planning live in a public repo at all?
