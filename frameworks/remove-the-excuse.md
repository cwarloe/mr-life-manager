# Remove the Excuse

**Source:** Dave Webster, [2009 garage clean-up document](../source-material/2009-10-26-dave-webster-garage-cleanup.md).
His words:

> Make sure that dump is open the day you decide to organize. **We don't want any
> excuses that the dump isn't open.**

## The idea

A prep list looks like it exists to gather supplies. It doesn't. It exists to
**close off every reason a person will stop halfway.**

Dave's garage document spends its entire first section on this, before a single
instruction about cleaning anything. Is the donation site open that day? Do you
have a vehicle? Is there somewhere for the trash to go? Are the shelves already
bought and measured for?

None of that is cleaning. All of it is removing an exit.

## Why it works

People rarely abandon a job because it's too hard. They abandon it at the first
**legitimate-sounding reason to stop** — and a genuine obstacle is the most
legitimate-sounding reason there is. "I couldn't finish, the dump was closed" is
true, unarguable, and ends the project.

The obstacle didn't cause the failure. It gave the failure permission.

So you find the permissions in advance and revoke them.

## The second half: name the ones you can't remove

Some excuses have no logistical fix, because they're internal. Dave handles those
by listing them, plainly, under the heading **"Common excuses at this stage"**:

> 1. This will take too long
> 2. There is too much to do, glazed eyes start appearing
> 3. I need to take a nap
> 4. I forgot where the trash can is
> 5. I lost the directions to Good Will and the Salvation Army
> 6. "I will do it tomorrow"  yeah right!  Get your ass moving.
> 7. Am sure you can come up with some good ones.

Naming them in advance is the whole intervention. An excuse works by arriving
disguised as a fact. Once you've read it on a list before you started, you
recognize the voice when it shows up, and it stops sounding like information.

Note what he does with overwhelm — item 2, the glazed eyes. He files it as an
excuse, not a diagnosis. That's a deliberate choice, and it's the same choice
[Principle 11](../docs/vision/principles.md) makes: the feeling is real, and it
is still not a reason the job doesn't get done.

## How we apply it

**In every guide with a prep step:** the prep step's job is excuse removal.
Written that way, it stops being a shopping list and starts being load-bearing.
Ask of each item — *which specific way of quitting does this close off?* If the
answer is "none," it's clutter.

**In the timing:** Dave checks whether the dump is open *on the day you chose*.
Not generally. A prep item that isn't tied to the actual attempt hasn't removed
anything.

**In the bounding:** *"Your plan is to have this done in one day or less, so
start early in the morning."* An unbounded job generates excuses faster than you
can close them. Give the work an end.

**In the finish:** *"3. Go get a beer."* There is a terminus, and it is
observable. Half the reason people stop mid-job is that nothing ever told them
they were done.
