# Do It Now

## First, what this does not mean

**"Do it now" does not mean do everything immediately.** Taken literally, it is
terrible advice, and it's worth being blunt about why:

- You become an interrupt handler. Every arriving thing wins, regardless of
  importance.
- Nothing that requires uninterrupted attention ever gets finished.
- Urgency quietly replaces priority. The loudest task wins, not the best one.
- It ends in burnout, because "now" has no capacity limit and life does.

A person who genuinely does everything the moment it appears is not organized.
They're reactive, and they're usually exhausted.

## What it actually means

> **Decide now. Do later — on purpose.**

The cost of an incoming task isn't the doing. It's the **carrying**: the item
sits in your head, resurfaces at random, gets re-evaluated a dozen times, and
generates anxiety proportional to how long it stays undecided.

*Deciding* is what has to happen immediately. The decision takes seconds and ends
the carrying. The doing gets scheduled according to actual priority.

**The corollary that makes it practical:** when a decision comes back "do it,"
and it's genuinely small, then yes — do it right now, because scheduling a
two-minute task costs more than doing it.

## The four outcomes

Every incoming thing — a task, a message, a piece of mail, a request, a thought —
gets routed to exactly one of four destinations. Immediately. Every time.

```
          Something arrives
                 │
        ┌────────┴────────┐
        │  Does it matter? │
        └────────┬────────┘
           no    │    yes
        ┌────────┘    └────────┐
        ▼                      ▼
   ┌─────────┐         ┌───────────────┐
   │ DISCARD │         │ Am I the one? │
   └─────────┘         └───────┬───────┘
                    no ┌───────┴───────┐ yes
                       ▼               ▼
                 ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────────┐
                 │ DELEGATE │   │ Under ~2 min │
                 └──────────┘   │ and can I    │
                                │ do it now?   │
                                └──────┬───────┘
                              yes ┌────┴────┐ no
                                  ▼         ▼
                             ┌────────┐ ┌──────────┐
                             │ DO NOW │ │ SCHEDULE │
                             └────────┘ └──────────┘
```

### DISCARD — when it doesn't need to exist

**Do this first**, always. Most people never seriously consider it, and it's the
cheapest option available.

**Discard when:**
- It doesn't actually matter, and won't in a week
- It's someone else's expectation you never agreed to
- The consequence of not doing it is acceptable
- It's been on your list for months and you keep not doing it *(that's a
  decision — make it explicit)*
- It's junk mail, a marketing email, or a notification
- It's a "someday" idea with no real intention behind it
- Doing it would cost more than the problem it solves

**Say no out loud.** A declined request is finished. A request you never answered
follows you around and eventually costs a relationship.

**The stale-list rule:** anything on a list for 90 days is telling you something.
Either schedule it this week or delete it. There is no third option that isn't
just guilt storage.

### DELEGATE — when someone else should own it

Not just for people with staff. Delegation in ordinary life includes:

- **A household member** — dividing responsibilities explicitly
  (→ [everything-has-a-place.md](everything-has-a-place.md) §6)
- **A professional** — a mechanic, a plumber, an accountant. Paying someone is
  delegation, and it's often correct.
- **Automation** — autopay, recurring orders, calendar reminders, filters.
  **The best delegation available, because the recipient never forgets.**
- **The person who actually owns it** — a landlord, an employer, a company's
  support line. A surprising amount of what people carry is not theirs.

**Rules for delegating well**
- Hand over the whole loop, not just the visible step
- Say when it's needed
- Then actually let go — supervising a delegated task costs nearly what doing it
  would
- Automate before you delegate to a human. It's cheaper and more reliable.

**Diagnostic:** for anything recurring you've done more than five times, ask
whether it could be automated. Most bills, orders, and reminders can be.

### DO NOW — when the overhead exceeds the task

If it takes about two minutes or less, and you can do it here, do it now. Writing
it down, scheduling it, remembering it, and returning to it costs more than two
minutes.

**Classic two-minute wins**
- Hang the coat instead of draping it
- Rinse the dish instead of setting it down
- Put the item back where it came from
- Answer the one-line message
- Wipe the counter while the pan cools
- Recycle the junk mail on the walk in from the mailbox
- Put the receipt where receipts go

**The compounding argument.** Two minutes now is very often twenty minutes later:

| Now | Later |
|---|---|
| Rinse the pan while it's warm | Scrub baked-on residue |
| Hang one coat | Sort a chair pile |
| Treat the stain immediately | Own a permanently stained shirt |
| Wipe the spill | Mop a sticky floor |
| Reply "yes, Thursday works" | Untangle a scheduling mess |

**But respect the limits:**
- **Not during focused work.** A two-minute task that costs fifteen minutes of
  re-entry is not a two-minute task. Capture it and continue.
- **Not when it's the twentieth in a row.** Twenty two-minute tasks is a
  forty-minute block. Batch them.
- **Not when it's really a bigger task in disguise.** "Just reply to this email"
  that requires a decision, a lookup, and a document is not two minutes.

### SCHEDULE — when it matters but not right now

The default for most real work. Scheduling isn't procrastination; it's
**assigning the task a place in time** — the time-domain version of *everything
has a place*.

**Rules for scheduling that works**

1. **A specific destination.** A named list, a calendar slot, or a recurring
   system. Never "later," which is not a place.
2. **Time-bound things go on the calendar.** If missing it has a consequence, it
   is not a list item.
3. **Estimate honestly, then add half.** Chronic under-estimation is why
   schedules collapse and people conclude they're bad at this.
4. **Batch by context.** All errands together, all calls together, all admin
   together. Context-switching is the hidden tax.
5. **Leave slack.** A fully committed calendar has no capacity for reality.
   Roughly 60–70% committed is sustainable; 100% guarantees failure.
6. **Recurring things get a recurring system**, not a re-decision each time. This
   is where scheduling becomes prevention →
   [preventative-vs-crisis.md](preventative-vs-crisis.md).

**Scheduling fails when** the destination is vague, the estimate is fantasy, the
day is already full, or nothing ever reviews the list.

## Capture: the precondition

None of this works without a **single, trusted capture point** — one place where
everything lands the instant you think of it, before it gets routed.

- One inbox. Not five. Paper notebook, phone note, index card — the tool matters
  far less than there being exactly one.
- Always available, including at 2 a.m. and in the car.
- Emptied regularly. An inbox never processed becomes a pile, and a pile is where
  trust dies.

**Why capture matters more than any other step:** if you don't trust the system to
hold things, your brain refuses to let go — and keeps rehearsing every open item
to make sure it isn't forgotten. That rehearsal *is* the mental load. Capture is
what stops it.

---

## The review: what keeps it alive

Any system built on this decays without review. Two rhythms:

**Daily (5 minutes)** — empty the capture point, route everything through the four
outcomes, look at tomorrow.

**Weekly (20–30 minutes)** — empty every inbox, review all lists, review the
calendar two weeks out, discard the stale, plan the coming week.

**The weekly review is the highest-leverage half hour in the entire framework.**
Everything else is technique; this is what makes it a system rather than a good
intention.

## Worked examples

**Mail arrives.** Standing at the recycling bin: junk → discard on the spot. Bill
→ if on autopay, file it; if not, either pay now (two minutes) or calendar it.
Something requiring action → capture it, file the paper. *Nothing gets stacked.*

**A text asking for a favor.** Can't do it → say no now. Can → yes and calendar
it. Need to think → "let me check and get back to you tomorrow," and capture the
reminder. *The one thing you never do is leave it unanswered while you decide.*

**You notice the smoke detector chirping.** Have batteries → do now, two minutes.
No batteries → add to the list, and schedule the whole-house battery swap
annually so it never happens again.

**A large project appears.** Never route a project through this. Decide the **next
physical action**, and route *that*. "Do taxes" isn't actionable; "find last
year's return" is.

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal interpretation** | Doing everything on arrival, finishing nothing | Deciding is what's immediate, not doing |
| **Never discarding** | Lists growing forever | Force the 90-day decision |
| **Vague scheduling** | "Later" and "soon" | A named list or a calendar slot |
| **No capture point** | Carrying it all in your head | One inbox, always available |
| **No review** | The system silently rots | Weekly, non-negotiable |
| **Two-minute abuse** | Constant interruption of real work | Batch; capture during focus |
| **Routing projects** | A stalled item that never moves | Route the next action instead |
| **Perfectionism** | Won't start what can't be finished perfectly | Decide, then do the 80% version |

---

## The one-line version

> **When something arrives, decide immediately: discard it, delegate it, do it if
> it's tiny, or schedule it somewhere real. Never leave it undecided.**

The undecided pile is the enemy. Not the messy house — the pile of things you
haven't decided about, which lives partly on your counter and partly in your head.
