# Everything Has a Place

## The slogan is the easy part

"A place for everything and everything in its place" is old advice, and as
usually stated it's useless — because it describes a *result* without explaining
the *mechanism*.

The mechanism is this:

> **Disorder is not a cleaning problem. It is an unmade-decision problem.**

An object with no assigned home forces a decision every single time it's set
down. Where does this go? *I don't know.* So it goes on the counter. The counter
is not a location — it's a **deferred decision**, and deferred decisions
accumulate.

A home that "gets messy fast" usually isn't dirty. It's full of unmade decisions.

**The framework's actual claim:** make each decision **once**, permanently, in
advance. Then putting something away requires no thinking at all, and putting
things away becomes automatic instead of effortful.

---

## The four tests of a good place

A place is properly defined when it passes all four:

1. **Named.** You can say where it goes out loud. "Somewhere in the kitchen" is
   not a place. "The left drawer by the stove" is.
2. **Singular.** One home, not several. Two homes means a search every time and
   two piles neither of which is complete.
3. **Near the point of use.** The distance between where a thing is used and
   where it lives is the tax you pay every time. Scissors belong where you cut,
   not in a designated Scissors Location across the house.
4. **Has capacity.** A full place isn't a place. If putting something away
   requires first removing something else, the system fails on the first tired
   day.

**The test that matters most:** can someone *else* in the household put it away
correctly without asking? If not, the place isn't defined — it's just a habit
living in one person's head.

## Applying it to six things

The framework's real power is that it isn't about objects. The same failure — no
defined home, so it lives in your head — shows up in six domains.

### 1. Physical possessions

**Failure mode:** items with no home, homes too far from use, homes too full,
drop zones absorbing everything, duplicates because you couldn't find the first
one.

**How to do it**

- Work by **category and frequency**, not by room. Daily-use items get prime
  real estate: waist-to-eye height, front of the shelf, no door to open.
  Rarely-used items go high, low, or out of the way. Most people invert this
  without noticing.
- **Store at the point of use.** Cleaning supplies in the room they clean, not in
  one central closet. Yes, buy a second set of basics — it costs $12 and buys a
  behavior change.
- **Reduce steps.** Every lid, door, bin, and stack is friction. An open basket
  gets used; a labeled bin inside a closed cabinet behind a door does not.
- **Give drop zones a job.** A landing spot by the door is not a failure of
  discipline — it's a real need. Design it: a tray for keys, a hook for the bag,
  a bowl for pocket contents, a slot for incoming mail. An *undesigned* drop zone
  swallows the house; a *designed* one protects it.
- **Watch where things actually land.** Where objects accumulate is data about
  where their home should be. Don't fight the pattern — relocate the home to
  match it.
- **Capacity is a real constraint.** If a place is chronically overfull, either
  it's the wrong place or there's too much stuff. Both are fixable; ignoring it
  is not.

**Diagnostic:** stand in a room and pick up five random objects. If you can't
instantly name where each belongs, that's the work.

### 2. Digital information

Files, photos, notes, emails, and downloads follow exactly the same rules — and
fail worse, because digital clutter is invisible and therefore never triggers the
discomfort that prompts a cleanup.

**How to do it**

- **One primary location per type.** One place for documents, one for photos, one
  for notes. Three cloud services with overlapping contents is the digital
  equivalent of four junk drawers.
- **Shallow beats deep.** Two or three levels of folders. Deep hierarchies fail
  because filing becomes a decision, and decisions get deferred — to the Desktop.
- **Name so it sorts and searches.** `2026-03-14 Lease Renewal.pdf` beats
  `scan_0043.pdf` forever. Date-first naming sorts chronologically for free.
- **Downloads is a hallway, not a room.** Everything passes through; nothing
  lives there. Empty it on a schedule.
- **Inbox is not storage.** Email arriving is not the same as email being filed.
  → [do-it-now.md](do-it-now.md)
- **If it isn't backed up, it doesn't have a place.** A single copy on one device
  is not stored; it's at risk. Two copies, one of them off-site.

**Diagnostic:** how long to find last year's tax return? Under a minute is
working. Over five means there's no system.

### 3. Tasks

A task with no home lives in your memory, and memory is the worst container you
own — it leaks, it prompts you at 2 a.m., and it fails hardest on your worst day.

**How to do it**

- **One capture point.** Somewhere everything lands the moment you think of it.
  Paper, app, notebook — the tool is nearly irrelevant; having exactly one is
  everything.
- **Then a defined destination.** Every captured item goes to a list, a calendar,
  someone else, or the trash. → [do-it-now.md](do-it-now.md)
- **Time-bound tasks live on the calendar, not on a list.** A list is a menu; a
  calendar is a commitment.
- **Recurring tasks live in a recurring system,** never in memory. This is where
  the framework meets
  [preventative-vs-crisis.md](preventative-vs-crisis.md).
- **A regular review is the maintenance.** Any task system decays without a
  weekly look at it. Without review, it becomes an archive of guilt.

**Diagnostic:** if you woke up with amnesia, would your system still tell you
what you owe people this week?

### 4. Documents

The highest-stakes application. Most people can absorb a messy closet. Nobody can
absorb losing a birth certificate the week they need it.

**A working taxonomy**

| Tier | Contents | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Vital / irreplaceable** | Birth certificate, SS card, passport, marriage & divorce records, titles, deeds, military DD-214, naturalization papers | Fireproof safe or safe deposit box. Scanned copies stored separately. |
| **Active reference** | Lease, insurance policies, warranties, current-year tax docs, medical records | One accessible file box or drawer, by category |
| **Retention-period** | Past tax returns and supporting documents, major receipts, closed-account records | Archive box or folder, labeled by year |
| **Transient** | Bills, statements, junk, offers | Processed on arrival, then shredded or recycled — never stored |

**Rules that make it work**

- **Mail gets processed, not stacked.** A stack of mail is a stack of unmade
  decisions with deadlines attached.
- **Scan the vital tier.** A photo of your passport in secure storage has saved
  more trips than any filing cabinet.
- **Know retention periods** so you can throw things away with confidence.
  Keeping everything forever is the same failure as keeping nothing — you can't
  find anything either way.
- **Somebody else should be able to find these.** In an emergency, a spouse, a
  parent, or an executor needs to locate your documents without you. Write down
  where they are.

**Diagnostic:** if your apartment flooded tonight, could you replace your
identity? If a family member had to handle your affairs tomorrow, would they
know where to look?

### 5. Money

Money without assigned places doesn't get lost — it gets *spent*. Undesignated
money is available money, and available money disappears.

**How to do it**

- **Give every dollar a destination:** spending, bills, savings, giving, buffer.
  This is what a budget actually is, stripped of spreadsheets.
- **Separate accounts are physical places for money.** A distinct savings account
  works because moving money out of it requires a decision, and that friction is
  the whole point. → [principles.md](../docs/vision/principles.md) §5, in reverse.
- **Bills have a place: autopay.** A bill dependent on you remembering has no
  place. Autopay is a home for a recurring obligation.
- **The buffer is a place, not a feeling.** "I have some savings" is not a
  system. A named amount in a named account for a named purpose is.
- **Irregular expenses need a home too.** Car registration, insurance premiums,
  and Christmas are not surprises — they're annual, and setting aside monthly
  gives them a place before they arrive.

**Diagnostic:** can you name where every dollar of last month's income went — in
categories, not line items? If not, money has no places.

### 6. Responsibilities

The subtlest and most relationally expensive application. In any shared
household, unassigned responsibility doesn't get divided — it gets absorbed by
whoever notices first. That person becomes the household's memory, and burns out.

**How to do it**

- **Assign ownership explicitly.** Not "we both do the kitchen" but "you own the
  dishes, I own the trash and the floor." Ambiguity is where resentment grows.
- **Name the invisible work.** Noticing that supplies are low, tracking birthdays,
  scheduling appointments, knowing what's in the fridge — this is real labor. It
  is nearly always uncounted, and it is nearly always the source of the fight.
- **Write it down.** A household agreement isn't bureaucratic; it converts an
  ongoing negotiation into a settled decision. → the Household Operating
  Agreement in [product-ladder.md](../docs/business/product-ladder.md).
- **Own the whole loop, not the task.** "Take out the trash" means noticing it's
  full, taking it out, replacing the bag, and knowing when you're out of bags.
  Assigning only the visible step leaves the noticing with someone else.
- **Give every responsibility a review point.** Things change. An agreement
  nobody revisits becomes an agreement nobody honors.

**Diagnostic:** ask each person in the household to list what they're responsible
for. Compare. The gaps and the overlaps are the entire conversation.

## Implementation: how to actually do this

Don't reorganize your whole life. It fails every time. Work one boundary at a
time.

**Step 1 — Pick one zone.** One drawer, one counter, one folder, one category of
task. Small enough to finish in under an hour.

**Step 2 — Empty it completely.** You cannot design around what's already there;
you'll just rearrange the existing assumptions.

**Step 3 — Sort by frequency**, not by type. Daily / weekly / occasionally /
never. "Never" is a decision waiting to be made.

**Step 4 — Assign homes by the four tests.** Named, singular, near use, has
capacity.

**Step 5 — Make putting away easier than not.** If the correct action takes more
effort than the incorrect one, the design has already lost.

**Step 6 — Tell the household.** A place only one person knows about is not a
place.

**Step 7 — Live with it a week, then adjust.** Where things pile up is where the
design was wrong. That's information, not failure.

## Failure modes to avoid

| Failure | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Over-engineering** | Organizing is fun; maintaining isn't | Fewer categories, fewer containers, less labeling |
| **Aesthetic over functional** | It photographs well | If it adds a step, it's costing you |
| **Homes too far from use** | Filed by category, not behavior | Move the home to where the thing gets used |
| **Places at 100% capacity** | Optimizing for storage volume | Leave slack; a full place isn't a place |
| **Undocumented places** | It lives in one person's head | Write it down or tell people |
| **Doing it all at once** | Motivation spike | One zone at a time |
| **Buying containers first** | Shopping feels like progress | Assign homes first; buy only what's still missing |

**The container trap deserves its own warning:** buying organizing products
before deciding what goes where is the most common and most expensive mistake in
this entire domain. It converts a decision problem into a shopping problem, which
feels productive and changes nothing.

---

## Why this matters beyond tidiness

Every application above removes something from your head and puts it somewhere
reliable. That is the actual product: **fewer open loops.**

A home where everything has a place isn't just cleaner. It's a home where you
aren't quietly tracking a hundred unresolved things — which is the difference
between a space that drains you and one you can invite someone into.
